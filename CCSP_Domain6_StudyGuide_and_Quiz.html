6.1 Legal requirements and unique risks Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 9, Legal and Compliance Issues (legal requirements, eDiscovery, and forensics sections).

Conflicting law, cloud legal risk, eDiscovery, and forensics

Conflicting international legislation

Laws on data protection, privacy, and cybersecurity vary significantly between countries and regions, and organisations operating globally can find themselves subject to requirements that genuinely conflict. One jurisdiction may compel disclosure of data that another jurisdiction forbids transferring. Cloud sharpens this because data moves and replicates across regions by default unless deliberately restricted.

Legal risks specific to cloud computing

These arise from the nature of cloud itself, where data is stored, processed, and managed remotely on third-party servers. Key risks include data privacy and protection obligations following the data wherever it goes; uncertainty over which jurisdiction's law applies; loss of direct control over data handling; contractual gaps where the provider's standard terms do not meet the customer's regulatory duties; and difficulty exercising rights such as audit or evidence collection on infrastructure the customer does not own.

Legal frameworks and guidelines

The framework is the set of laws, regulations, standards, and ethical guidelines governing how data is handled, processed, and stored, designed to protect individual privacy and secure sensitive data. A CCSP is not expected to give legal advice but must recognise which regimes apply and when to involve counsel.

eDiscovery

eDiscovery is the process of identifying, collecting, preserving, and reviewing electronic information for use as evidence in legal proceedings. In cloud it is complicated by not controlling the infrastructure, by data being dispersed across regions, and by the need to extract only the requesting party's data without capturing other tenants' information. ISO/IEC 27050 provides the standard for electronic discovery, and CSA guidance addresses the cloud-specific dimension. A legal hold suspends normal retention and deletion for data within its scope until proceedings conclude.

Forensics requirements

Forensic requirements are the standards, practices, and tools for investigating and analysing digital evidence after an incident, essential for establishing cause and extent, identifying perpetrators, and taking remedial action. The named standards form a family: ISO/IEC 27037 covers identification, collection, acquisition, and preservation of digital evidence; 27041 addresses assurance of investigation methods; 27042 covers analysis and interpretation; and 27043 covers incident investigation principles and processes. CSA guidance again supplies the cloud-specific layer.

Watch this in the exam

Keep the standards separated. ISO/IEC 27050 is eDiscovery. The 27037/27041/27042/27043 family is digital forensics. ISO/IEC 27018 is PII protection in public clouds and belongs to privacy, not forensics. Questions routinely offer all three groups together.

6.2 Privacy issues Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 9, Legal and Compliance Issues (privacy, PII, and jurisdictional differences sections).

Contractual versus regulated data, national law, and privacy assessments

Contractual versus regulated private data

This distinction is heavily tested. Contractual private data is protected because an agreement between parties says so; the obligation arises from the contract, and breach is a contractual matter with commercial consequences. Regulated private data is protected by law regardless of what any contract says; the obligation arises from statute, and breach carries regulatory penalties that no contract can waive. PHI under HIPAA and personal data under GDPR are regulated. A confidentiality clause covering a client's commercial information is contractual.

Example

A bank's contract with a supplier requires both to keep pricing terms confidential, which is contractual private data. The same bank's customer records are regulated private data under UK GDPR. If the pricing leaks, the remedy is contractual. If the customer records leak, the regulator becomes involved regardless of what any contract said.

Country-specific legislation

  • GDPR, the comprehensive European regulation centred on consent, rights of access, and the right to be forgotten, with extraterritorial reach over anyone processing EU residents' data.
  • HIPAA, governing protected health information in the United States, including its Breach Notification Rule.
  • FERPA, governing student education records in the United States.
  • PIPEDA, Canada's private-sector personal information law.
  • Digital Personal Data Protection Act, India's personal data legislation.

Jurisdictional differences

Some regions such as the EU maintain robust comprehensive frameworks emphasising individual rights, strict consent, and strong enforcement. Others take a sectoral approach, regulating particular industries rather than personal data generally, and some have limited protection. An organisation operating across borders must satisfy the strictest applicable regime for each dataset rather than assuming one standard suffices.

Standard privacy requirements

ISO/IEC 27018 is the code of practice for protecting PII in public clouds acting as PII processors. GAPP, the Generally Accepted Privacy Principles, provides a recognised set of privacy principles. GDPR itself functions as a standard many organisations align to globally.

Privacy Impact Assessments

A PIA is a proactive assessment conducted before processing begins, identifying privacy risks in a proposed activity and the measures to address them. It is forward-looking, which distinguishes it from breach notification, an obligation that arises after something has gone wrong.

6.3 Audit process and methodologies Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 9, Legal and Compliance Issues (audit controls, report types, scope restrictions, gap analysis, and policy sections).

Audit controls, assurance challenges, report types, and policy

Internal and external audit controls

Organisations moving to cloud need ongoing assurance that controls remain in place. Internal audit serves as a third line of defence, independent of the operational teams it examines. External audit brings independent third-party attestation that carries weight with customers and regulators. Both are needed: internal audit provides continuous visibility, external audit provides credible independent assurance.

Assurance challenges of virtualisation and cloud

Virtualisation introduces a complex management architecture that adds an abstraction layer auditors must understand. Further challenges include limited physical access to infrastructure, multi-tenancy making it impossible to examine shared components without touching other tenants, dynamic and elastic resources meaning the environment audited today differs from tomorrow's, and dependence on provider-supplied evidence rather than direct observation.

Audit report types

  • SOC 1, reporting on controls relevant to financial reporting.
  • SOC 2, reporting against the Trust Service Criteria covering security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy. It is the report security teams actually need.
  • SOC 3, a high-level public summary of a SOC 2 without the detailed controls and test results, suitable for general distribution but too thin for real assurance work.
  • Type I versus Type II, applying to both SOC 1 and SOC 2. Type I assesses whether controls are suitably designed at a single point in time. Type II assesses whether they operated effectively over a period, typically six to twelve months.

SSAE 18 is the US attestation standard under which SOC reports are produced, and ISAE 3402 is its international equivalent.

Watch this in the exam

SOC 1 is financial reporting, SOC 2 is the security and privacy criteria, SOC 3 is the public summary. Type I is design at a point in time, Type II is effectiveness over a period. If a scenario asks for evidence controls actually worked, only a Type II answers it.

Restrictions of audit scope statements

Audit scope is deliberately bounded, and understanding the boundaries matters as much as reading the findings. Restrictions include asset restrictions, where critical or sensitive assets are explicitly excluded to protect them; accepted times and time periods, scheduling audits when disruption is least; and geographic or service limitations covering only certain regions or offerings. A clean report over a narrow scope proves far less than its cover page suggests.

Gap analysis

Gap analysis compares the current control state against a required or desired state, identifying the difference to be closed. It draws on control analysis, baselines, and risk and control self-assessment, and it typically precedes a formal audit so that known gaps are remediated first.

Audit planning

Planning sets the foundation for the engagement: understanding the audit objectives, defining scope, assessing risk to focus effort where it matters, allocating resources, and agreeing the schedule and evidence requirements with the parties involved.

Internal ISMS, controls system, and policies

An internal Information Security Management System provides the governing structure for security, most commonly following ISO/IEC 27001. The internal information security controls system is the set of controls implemented within it. Policies operate at two levels: organisational policies such as the overarching information security policy expressing management commitment, and functional policies addressing specific areas such as access control, acceptable use, and data classification. Cloud computing policy governs how cloud services may be selected, configured, and used.

Stakeholders and specialised compliance

Identifying and involving relevant stakeholders is crucial to cloud adoption, since engaging them surfaces perspectives, concerns, and requirements early. Highly regulated industries carry additional layers: NERC CIP for electric utilities, HIPAA and HITECH for healthcare, and PCI DSS for payment card handling, layered on top of jurisdictional requirements.

Impact of distributed IT models

Distributed IT brings advantages but must not undermine communication, coordination, management, and governance. Diverse geographic locations mean crossing legal jurisdictions, and dispersed teams and systems make consistent policy enforcement and clear accountability harder to maintain.

6.4 Cloud and enterprise risk management Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 9, Legal and Compliance Issues and Chapter 10, Cloud Vendor Management (risk management, data roles, and risk framework sections).

Provider risk programmes, data roles, transparency, treatment, and frameworks

Assessing a provider's risk management programme

Because the customer hosts its services and data in the provider's environment, the provider's risk management processes and its risk acceptance decisions directly affect the customer's own security posture. Assessment should cover the provider's controls, methodologies, policies, risk profile, and risk appetite. A provider that accepts risks the customer would not accept has effectively made that decision on the customer's behalf.

Data roles

This is among the most frequently tested distinctions in the certification:

  • Data owner or controller, the party that determines the purposes and means of processing. Accountability for the data rests here and cannot be outsourced.
  • Data processor, the party that processes data on the controller's behalf and under its instructions.
  • Data custodian, the party with day-to-day technical responsibility for safeguarding the data, implementing the controls the owner requires.
  • Data steward, responsible for data quality, meaning, and fitness for business use.
  • Data subject, the individual the personal data relates to.
Example

A bank decides what customer data it collects and why, making it the controller. Its cloud provider stores and processes that data under the bank's instructions, making it the processor. The bank's database administrators implement the encryption and access controls day to day, acting as custodians. The customer is the data subject. When the regulator asks who is accountable, the answer is the bank, regardless of which party's systems held the data.

Watch this in the exam

Owner and controller determine purpose. Custodian and processor act on instruction. Accountability stays with the controller even when processing is fully outsourced, which is why a scenario asking who is accountable after a provider breach almost always answers with the customer.

Regulatory transparency requirements

Transparency obligations require organisations to disclose certain matters to regulators, individuals, or markets. Breach notification is the principal example: the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule requires covered entities and their business associates to notify following a breach of unsecured protected health information, and GDPR requires notification to the supervisory authority, generally within 72 hours of becoming aware of a qualifying breach. Sarbanes-Oxley imposes disclosure and internal control requirements on financial reporting for listed companies.

Risk treatment

Risk measures the threats an organisation, its systems, and its data are exposed to. Risk assessment evaluates vulnerability, identifies threats, judges the likelihood of a threat succeeding, and considers mitigations. The five treatments are:

  • Avoid, not undertaking the activity that creates the risk.
  • Mitigate, applying controls to reduce likelihood or impact.
  • Transfer, shifting financial consequence to another party, typically insurance.
  • Share, distributing risk across parties through partnership or contractual allocation.
  • Accept, deliberately bearing the risk where treatment costs more than the exposure warrants.

Risk frameworks

The named frameworks are ISO 31000, providing generic risk management principles and guidelines applicable to any organisation; ENISA, the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity, whose cloud risk assessment work addresses cloud specifically; and NIST, particularly its cloud computing synopsis and recommendations alongside the Risk Management Framework.

Metrics and assessment of the risk environment

Risks must be described clearly enough to communicate both internally and externally, which requires an agreed set of risk management metrics with probability and impact assessed separately for each risk. Assessment of the risk environment extends beyond a single system to cover the service, the vendor, the infrastructure, and the business, because risk in any of these reaches the others.

6.5 Outsourcing and cloud contract design Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 10, Cloud Vendor Management (business requirements, vendor management, contract management, and supply chain sections).

Agreements, vendor management, contract terms, and supply chain

Business requirements

Before contracting with a provider, an organisation should evaluate its own needs, which form the foundation of its cloud strategy. Key organisational assets must be identified and assessed for suitability in cloud environments. Three instruments define the relationship:

  • Service Level Agreement (SLA), defining measurable service commitments such as availability and support response, with remedies where they are not met.
  • Master Service Agreement (MSA), the overarching contract setting general terms governing the whole relationship.
  • Statement of Work (SOW), specifying the particular deliverables, tasks, and timelines for a defined piece of work under the MSA.
Watch this in the exam

The MSA is the umbrella agreement, the SOW defines specific work under it, and the SLA sets measurable performance commitments. Questions describing recurring general terms want the MSA; those describing defined deliverables want the SOW; those describing uptime percentages and credits want the SLA.

Vendor management

Rapid growth in the provider market means many new entrants competing for share, and not all will prove durable. Vendor management covers vendor assessments of security, financial, and operational fitness; vendor lock-in risks, where proprietary dependency makes switching impractical; vendor viability, the provider's financial stability and prospects as a going concern; and escrow, placing source code or critical materials with a neutral third party so the customer can continue operating if the vendor fails.

Contract management

Effective contract management requires appropriate governance and covers monitoring contract performance, addressing ongoing needs, adhering to terms, and managing incidents, outages, discrepancies, and violations of contractual obligations. The terms the outline names:

  • Right to audit, permitting the customer to verify the provider's controls independently.
  • Metrics and definitions, so that terms such as availability mean the same thing to both parties.
  • Termination, setting out how the relationship ends and what happens to data.
  • Litigation and assurance, covering dispute handling and the evidence the provider must supply.
  • Compliance and access to cloud and data, ensuring the customer can meet its own regulatory duties and retrieve its data.
  • Cyber risk insurance, allocating financial consequence.
  • Data ownership, stating unambiguously that the customer's data remains the customer's.
  • Security requirements, specifying the controls the provider must maintain.

Supply chain management

Organisations invest heavily in protecting key assets and intellectual property, and changes in supply chain practice can put that at risk. The governing principle is that security is only as strong as the weakest link: a provider's own dependencies become the customer's exposure, even where no direct contract exists. ISO/IEC 27036 is the standard for information security in supplier relationships, covering how security requirements are defined, agreed, and monitored across the chain.

Example

An energy company contracts with a SaaS vendor that subcontracts data processing to a smaller analytics firm. The energy company has no contract with that firm, yet a breach there exposes its data just as directly. Assessing only the immediate vendor leaves the actual weakest link unexamined, which is precisely what supply chain management exists to prevent.
