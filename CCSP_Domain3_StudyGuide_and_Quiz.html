3.1 Cloud infrastructure and platform components Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 5, Cloud Platform, Infrastructure, and Operational Security (physical environment, network, compute, virtualization, storage, and management plane sections).

The building blocks a cloud runs on

Cloud infrastructure is the physical and virtual resources inside data centres: compute nodes, storage systems, and networking hardware, together with the virtualisation software and management layer that turn them into a service. The outline names six components, and the exam expects you to know what each covers and who is responsible for it.

Physical environment

The server rooms, data centres, and physical locations where the infrastructure sits. Who owns this depends on the deployment model, which is a point worth remembering: in a private cloud built on premises, the organisation is effectively its own provider and owns the physical environment. In a community cloud, a community member often supplies the space and hardware. With a commercial public provider, the provider owns it entirely and the customer typically never sees it.

Example

A bank running a private cloud in its own data centre is responsible for the locks on the doors, the guards, and the fire suppression. The same bank using a public provider inherits all of that from the provider and can only verify it through audit reports, never by walking the floor.

Network and communications

The provider secures the networking components it houses. The customer manages any components in its own facilities. The public internet connecting the two is the primary area of concern, because neither party controls it. The provider must support secure methods such as HTTPS, encryption before transmission, and VPN access, and the customer is expected to actually use them. Both parties implement firewalls and related technologies on their own side of the boundary.

Compute

Compute capability is defined mainly by CPU, which determines processing power, and RAM, which holds data actively being processed and keeps latency low. What makes cloud compute distinctive is how it is allocated in a multi-tenant environment, through three mechanisms you should be able to distinguish:

  • Reservations, a guaranteed minimum of resource for a system, ensuring it always has what it needs to perform.
  • Shares, a relative priority weighting used to decide who gets contested resources when demand exceeds supply.
  • Limits, a ceiling preventing any one workload from consuming more than its allowed maximum.
Example

An energy company's monitoring workload has a reservation guaranteeing four CPU cores so it never starves during a busy period. A batch reporting job has a limit capping it at two cores so it cannot crowd out anything else. When both compete for whatever is left over, their shares values decide who wins.

Storage

Cloud storage spreads data across distributed and often replicated resources. The two primary types at infrastructure level are volume storage, which behaves like a traditional hard drive attached to a virtual machine and suits file systems and databases needing low latency, and object storage, which suits unstructured data such as media, backups, and analytics datasets. Two underlying technologies support them: RAID, improving data integrity and performance through redundancy across disks, and SAN, connecting storage devices over a separate high speed network for reliable, flexible capacity.

Virtualisation

Virtualisation is the backbone of every cloud hosting model. It is what makes resource pooling, on-demand self-service, and scalability possible in the first place: without the ability to carve physical hardware into isolated logical instances, none of the essential characteristics could be delivered. It also introduces the hypervisor as a high value target and tenant isolation as a security requirement.

Management plane

The management plane enables remote administration of one or many hosts, providing the functions that define cloud operation: creating, starting, and stopping virtual machines, provisioning storage, and configuring networking. It is the single most security-sensitive component in the infrastructure, because compromising it grants control over everything beneath it. A stolen credential for a virtual machine costs you one machine; a stolen management plane credential can cost you the entire environment.

Watch this in the exam

Reservations, shares, and limits are frequently swapped in distractors. Reservation is a guaranteed floor, limit is a hard ceiling, and shares are relative priority during contention. If a scenario describes a workload that must never be starved, it wants a reservation. If it describes a workload that must never run away with the host, it wants a limit.

3.2 Design a secure data center Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 5, Cloud Platform, Infrastructure, and Operational Security (logical, physical, and environmental design sections).

Logical, physical, environmental, and resilient design

Logical design

The logical layout is the abstract representation of the data centre, and in a public cloud it is what the customer actually builds using the provider's tools and services. Getting it right is central to security. It covers tenant partitioning and access control, and in practice includes access management, regulatory compliance monitoring, patch management, log analysis, and service configuration, with Identity and Access Management establishing the secure perimeter.

Consistency of configuration is the recurring theme. The business specifies how each authorised cloud service should be configured and used, and uniform configuration makes administration simpler and usually more secure. Deviations from the established pattern should not be approved without first assessing the risk the variance introduces.

Example

A bank defines one approved configuration pattern for its cloud storage buckets: encryption on, public access blocked, logging enabled. When a project team asks for an exception to allow public access for a marketing asset, the request goes through risk assessment before approval rather than being granted as a routine change. The pattern is what makes the exception visible.

Physical design

Physical design is normally the owner's responsibility, which in a public cloud means the provider. Multi-tenancy adds complexity, but otherwise a provider's data centre resembles an on-premises one. Two considerations dominate:

  • Location, which materially reduces disaster risk. Regions prone to natural disasters, civil unrest, or similar instability should be avoided.
  • Buy or build, and buy and hold, recognising that moving to a cloud data centre is a long-term commitment. The decision weighs data sensitivity, the legal regulations governing where data may be stored, and the availability of qualified staff for on-premises maintenance. Where law prevents data crossing borders, the customer may have to keep data on premises unless the provider's contract can guarantee compliance.

Environmental design

Except in private clouds, the provider owns environmental design, and it directly affects the availability of cloud resources. For customers, evaluating a vendor's environmental design is part of vendor risk analysis.

  • HVAC, heating, ventilation, and air conditioning. Every computing facility must prioritise electrical service and climate control. Local climate shapes the emphasis: a facility near the equator worries far more about cooling than heating. The key exam point is that an HVAC failure can take out computing resources just as completely as an electrical or communications outage, which is why HVAC capacity and redundancy belong in any provider evaluation.
  • Multi-vendor pathway connectivity, using multiple internet service providers or connectivity vendors so that a single carrier failure does not sever access. The problem is usually not the internet as a whole but the connectivity of one specific customer or provider, which is exactly what multiple independent pathways solve.
Example

A contractor severs a fibre run outside a data centre. With a single connectivity vendor, the facility is unreachable however healthy the servers inside are. With multi-vendor pathway connectivity over physically separate routes, traffic shifts to the second carrier and the outage is invisible to users.

Design for resilience

Resilience is the ability to adapt to changing conditions and recover from disruption, achieved by proactive design rather than reaction. The outline anchors it to power, HVAC, and connectivity, the three utilities whose loss stops a data centre regardless of anything else. Practical design actions include siting the facility away from known hazard regions, designing with flexibility, building reliable and simple systems, adding redundancy wherever possible, and applying passive design principles that keep working without active intervention.

Watch this in the exam

Match the design category to the concern. Tenant partitioning and access control are logical design. Site selection and the buy or build decision are physical design. HVAC and multi-vendor connectivity are environmental design. Redundant power, cooling, and connectivity are resilience. Questions often describe one concern and offer all four categories as options.

3.3 Risks associated with cloud infrastructure and platforms Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 4, Security in the Cloud (cloud risks and risk management sections) and Chapter 5.

Assessing, understanding, and treating cloud risk

Risk assessment

Industry bodies publish useful risk lists, but every organisation, whether providing or consuming cloud services, must conduct its own assessment, because risk depends on the organisation's own data, obligations, and tolerance. Risk assessment has two parts: identification, finding what could go wrong, and analysis, judging how likely it is and how much it would hurt.

The organisational and regulatory risks that recur in cloud adoption include lock-in, where dependence on a single provider reduces flexibility; governance, ensuring policies actually manage cloud resources effectively and compliantly; data security and privacy, protecting stored data from unauthorised access; and compliance and jurisdictional exposure arising from where data and processing sit.

Cloud vulnerabilities, threats, and attacks

The fundamental risk of cloud is its internet-based model: the service is reachable by anyone with a connection, which means attacks can originate anywhere in the world. Threats may target the customer, the provider, or another of the provider's customers, and may also stem from the geographic location of the data centre itself. Denial of service is the classic example of an attack whose effect can reach a customer who was never the intended target, because shared infrastructure means shared consequences.

A second category is the insider risk on the provider's side: the possibility of data theft by provider employees. Contractual agreements help, but prevention is stronger. The practical defence is encryption with the customer managing its own keys externally, so that data exposed at the provider is unreadable without keys the provider never holds.

Example

A volumetric denial of service attack aimed at one tenant saturates shared network capacity, degrading service for unrelated customers on the same infrastructure. None of them were targeted, and none of them could have prevented it, which is the essence of shared technology risk.

Risk treatment strategies

Once risk is identified and analysed, it must be treated. The five formal treatments are the ones to know precisely, because distractors trade on confusing them:

  • Avoid, eliminating the risk by not undertaking the activity at all.
  • Mitigate, applying controls that reduce likelihood or impact.
  • Transfer, shifting the financial consequence to another party, typically through insurance.
  • Share, distributing risk across parties, for example through a partnership or contractual allocation.
  • Accept, consciously deciding to bear the risk because treatment costs more than the exposure warrants.

Alongside the formal taxonomy, the practical mitigation measures emphasised for cloud infrastructure are data encryption at rest, two-factor authentication, eliminating shared accounts so actions are attributable to individuals, insisting on a well-defined shared responsibility model, and using standardised cloud assessment questionnaires when evaluating providers.

Watch this in the exam

Transfer moves the financial impact, usually via insurance, but the incident still happens and the reputational damage is still yours. Mitigate reduces likelihood or impact through controls. Accept is a deliberate documented decision, not the absence of one. A scenario describing cyber insurance is transfer, never mitigation.

3.4 Plan and implement security controls Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 5, Cloud Platform, Infrastructure, and Operational Security (physical and environmental protection, system and communication protection, IAM, and audit mechanisms sections).

Protecting systems, controlling access, and proving it happened

Physical and environmental protection

Site selection drives physical protection. Placing a data centre near water in a region subject to storms, flooding, tornadoes, or seismic activity is poor practice. The selection criteria include the ability to restrict physical access, an adequate available workforce, and reliable access to power and other essential resources. Network capacity is a baseline requirement for any data centre, and for a provider serving many clients across a wide geography these standards are often more demanding than for an on-premises facility.

System, storage, and communication protection

  • Policy and procedures, the documented foundation that makes every other control consistent and enforceable.
  • Security function isolation, separating security functions from other system operations so that compromising an application does not hand over the controls protecting it.
  • Denial of service protection, combining resilience, prompt detection and response, and effective bandwidth and capacity management.
  • Boundary protection, firewalls, intrusion detection and prevention, and related controls at the edges between trust zones.

For data in transit, the recommended protections are TLS, IPSec, and VPNs, ensuring transmitted data cannot be read or altered in flight.

Identification, authentication, and authorisation

Organisations either extend existing on-premises IAM into the cloud or adopt cloud-based IAM services, weighing ease of management, security requirements, and the prospect of single sign-on across systems. Provider-supplied IAM systems are an option but tie the organisation more closely to that provider. User education underpins all of it: teaching people not to reuse passwords and to recognise phishing protects the identity layer in a way no technical control fully can.

The shared responsibility model governs the split. Providers are generally responsible for securing the infrastructure supporting the services. Customers are responsible for the security of their own data within those services, including encryption at rest and in transit and the implementation of robust access controls.

Example

A telecom company extends its corporate identity provider into its cloud accounts so engineers use one set of credentials with single sign-on and central revocation. When an engineer leaves, disabling one account removes access everywhere, rather than leaving forgotten local accounts scattered across cloud services.

Audit mechanisms

The purpose of an audit is to confirm that enough risk controls exist and that they work. Audits are commonly driven by regulatory or compliance requirements, and they work best integrated into corporate governance and enterprise risk management, where legal, technical, jurisdictional, contractual, and compliance requirements can be consolidated in one place.

Two mechanisms carry a cloud-specific complication that the exam tests directly, and both follow the same pattern: how much you can collect depends on the service model and your contract.

  • Log collection. The central challenge in cloud is determining which logs the customer can actually access. In IaaS the customer has extensive access to operating system, virtual device, platform, and application logs. In PaaS that comprehensive access is reduced, since the provider operates the layers beneath the application. In SaaS the customer sees only what the provider chooses to expose.
  • Packet capture. The same constraint applies. In IaaS, capture on the customer's own virtual machine is normally achievable. In PaaS or SaaS, capability depends entirely on the provider, because the customer does not control the network appliances or the capture points.
  • Correlation, bringing events from multiple sources together so that patterns invisible in any single log become apparent.
Watch this in the exam

The audit visibility gradient is a reliable exam theme. Moving from IaaS toward SaaS, the customer's ability to collect logs and capture packets shrinks as the provider assumes more of the stack. If a scenario needs deep packet capture but the workload is SaaS, the honest answer involves the contract and the provider, not a technical control the customer can deploy alone.

3.5 Business continuity and disaster recovery Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 4, Security in the Cloud (Disaster Recovery and Business Continuity section), with supporting material in Chapter 5.

Strategy, objectives, and getting the plan tested

BC and DR protect against data being unavailable and the business processes depending on it being non-functional, both of which carry adverse consequences for the organisation. Business continuity is about keeping operating; disaster recovery is about restoring after a disruptive event.

Main components of a BCDR strategy

  • Location, where the recovery capability sits, and crucially far enough from the primary site that one event cannot take both.
  • Data replication, keeping a current copy of the data at the recovery location.
  • Functionality replication, ensuring the recovery site can actually run the workload, not merely hold the data.
  • Planning, preparing, and provisioning, arranging the capacity and procedures in advance rather than during the incident.
  • Failover capability, the mechanism that shifts operations to the recovery site.
  • Returning to normal, the failback to primary operations, which is regularly overlooked and frequently the harder half of the exercise.
Example

A maritime company replicates its tracking database to a second region and assumes it is covered. During a test, the failover succeeds but the application tier does not exist in the second region, so there is nothing to serve the data. Data replication without functionality replication produces a recoverable database and an unusable service.

Business requirements: RPO, RTO, and RSL

Three terms define recovery expectations, and the third is the one candidates most often miss:

  • Recovery Point Objective (RPO), how much data loss is tolerable, expressed as a period of time. It determines how much information must be restored and therefore how frequently data must be replicated or backed up.
  • Recovery Time Objective (RTO), how quickly a system must be operational again after a disruption.
  • Recovery Service Level (RSL), the proportion of normal service capacity that must be available during recovery. Recovery is rarely all or nothing: an organisation may accept running at a defined percentage of full capacity while operating from the recovery site.
Example

A bank sets an RPO of fifteen minutes, an RTO of two hours, and an RSL of sixty percent. Translated: it can lose at most the last fifteen minutes of transactions, must be serving customers again within two hours, and can run at sixty percent of normal capacity while in recovery, accepting degraded throughput but not an outage.

Creating, implementing, and testing the plan

Implementing a BCDR plan runs through defining the scope of what the plan covers, gathering requirements from the business, analysing the resulting plan, and risk assessment of the arrangements themselves, followed by implementation and, critically, testing. An untested plan is an assumption. Testing is what converts a document into a capability, and it routinely reveals gaps such as missing dependencies, stale credentials, or capacity that exists on paper but not in practice.

Watch this in the exam

Keep the three objectives separate. RPO is data loss measured in time. RTO is time to restore service. RSL is the percentage of capacity available during recovery. A scenario that meets its restoration deadline but loses more data than allowed has failed its RPO, not its RTO, and the corrective action belongs with replication frequency rather than failover speed.
