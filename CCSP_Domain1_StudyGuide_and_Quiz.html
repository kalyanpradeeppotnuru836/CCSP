1.1 Cloud computing concepts Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 1, Architectural Concepts (Cloud Characteristics, Cloud Computing Roles and Responsibilities, Cloud Computing Reference Architecture, Virtualization)

Essential characteristics of cloud computing

Cloud computing is defined by a short list of essential characteristics. These are the checklist that separates genuine cloud computing from traditional outsourced hosting that has simply been given a cloud label. If a service is missing several of these, it is worth questioning whether it is really cloud computing at all.

Watch this in the exam

The original NIST definition lists five essential characteristics. The current ISC2 exam outline lists six, adding multi-tenancy alongside the other five. Answer against the ISC2 list. If a question asks how many essential characteristics there are, or offers multi-tenancy as an option, multi-tenancy counts.

On-demand self-service

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Characteristics

A customer can provision computing resources, such as server time or network storage, automatically and without needing a person at the provider to approve or configure it manually.

Example

A developer at a bank needs a new database instance for testing. Instead of raising a ticket and waiting several days for an infrastructure team to provision it, the developer logs into the provider's console, selects a few options, and the database is running within minutes. No human at the provider was involved in approving the request.

Broad network access

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Characteristics

Capabilities are available over the network and accessed through standard mechanisms that work across different client platforms, including laptops, phones, and tablets.

Example

An engineer at an energy company can check a cloud hosted monitoring dashboard from an office laptop, a tablet on the plant floor, or a phone while on call. It is the same service, reachable the same way, regardless of the device used.

Resource pooling

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Characteristics

The provider's computing resources are pooled to serve multiple customers using a multi-tenant model, with physical and virtual resources dynamically assigned according to demand. The customer generally has no visibility into the exact physical location of those resources, although some providers allow the customer to specify a general location, such as a country or region.

Example

A telecom operator's billing platform and an unrelated company's website might both be running as virtual machines on the same physical server inside a provider's data centre, fully isolated from one another, without either customer knowing which physical machine they landed on.

Rapid elasticity and scalability

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Characteristics (see the Elasticity vs. Scalability sidebar)

Capabilities can be provisioned and released elastically, in some cases automatically, to scale outward and inward with demand. To the customer, the resources available for provisioning often appear effectively unlimited.

Example

A maritime tracking platform sees a spike in traffic when a major port congestion event is reported in the news. Automatic scaling rules add extra web servers within minutes to handle the load, then remove them again once traffic returns to normal. No one had to manually provision or decommission any of that capacity.

Example from the field

Industries with heavily concentrated demand provide the clearest illustrations of elasticity. Gambling operators earn a large share of their annual revenue in short windows around major sporting events, so their platforms must scale up sharply for a tournament weekend and back down immediately afterwards. Retail shows the same pattern: when Black Friday was a single day rather than a month of promotions, e-commerce platforms had to absorb an enormous one-day spike without paying for that capacity all year round. Paying only for what is used during the spike is the commercial case for elasticity.

Measured service

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Characteristics

Cloud systems automatically monitor and optimise resource use through a metering capability appropriate to the type of service, such as storage, processing, bandwidth, or active user accounts. Usage can be reported and controlled, giving both provider and customer visibility into consumption.

Example

A bank's monthly cloud invoice itemises exactly how much storage, compute time, and data transfer each business unit consumed, in the same way an electricity bill charges for actual usage rather than a flat fee regardless of consumption.

Common question

Is measured service simply the same thing as scalability? It is not. Measured service is the metering and billing of what you consume; scalability and elasticity are about how capacity grows and shrinks. There is a practical wrinkle for managed services: when a monitored environment scales up, the monitoring, detection, and remediation capability wrapped around it must scale with it, otherwise the newly added resources become the least watched part of the estate.

Multi-tenancy

Study guide: Ch 1 › Multitenancy

A single instance of the service, and the infrastructure beneath it, serves many independent customers at once. Each tenant's data and configuration are kept logically separate, so no tenant can see or affect another, even though they share the same underlying platform. Multi-tenancy is what makes the economics of cloud work, and it is also the origin of several risks unique to cloud, such as tenant isolation failures.

Example

A bank and a completely unrelated retailer both use the same SaaS payroll product. They log into the same application, running the same code, but each sees only its own employees and its own settings. The separation is enforced by the software and the platform, not by giving each customer its own physical server.

Why this matters for the exam

  • Questions often describe a scenario and ask which characteristic is being demonstrated, or which one is missing.
  • A common source of confusion is mixing up resource pooling, which describes the provider's multi-tenant backend, with rapid elasticity, which describes how quickly capacity grows or shrinks. They sound related but answer different questions.
  • If a service requires a phone call and a two week wait to add storage, it is failing the on-demand self-service test. That is a useful signal in a scenario question.
  • Do not confuse multi-tenancy with resource pooling. Resource pooling is about the provider dynamically assigning resources from a shared pool. Multi-tenancy is about multiple customers being served by the same service instance while remaining logically separated. They are closely related, and both appear in the ISC2 list.

Cloud computing roles

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Computing Roles and Responsibilities

ISC2 also expects familiarity with the roles involved in a cloud relationship, since exam questions sometimes test whether you can correctly identify who is doing what.

  • Cloud service customer, the organization or individual using the cloud service.
  • Cloud service provider, the organization delivering the cloud service.
  • Cloud service partner, a party that supports the delivery of the service, for example through consulting or implementation work, without necessarily owning the customer relationship.
  • Cloud service broker, an entity that adds value by aggregating, integrating, or customizing services from one or more providers before delivering the result to the customer.
  • Regulator, an authority that sets and enforces rules the cloud arrangement must comply with.
Example

A maritime company might use a cloud service broker that combines vessel tracking data from one provider with weather data from another, then presents a single integrated dashboard to the shipping company. The broker adds value through that integration rather than owning the underlying infrastructure.

Two of these roles reward closer attention: the partner role, which is broader than it first sounds, and the boundary between standards bodies and regulators, which the exam probes directly.

Example from the field

Rackspace's managed security arm, for example, ran a 24/7/365 security operations centre out of San Antonio, Texas, offering services such as threat hunting on top of the underlying cloud platforms. That is a cloud service partner in action, and specifically a managed security services provider (MSSP): a third party supporting the customer's use of cloud services without being the cloud provider itself.

Watch this in the exam

Keep regulators separate from standards bodies. A regulator enforces law: the ICO enforces the GDPR in the UK and is often described as the police of the GDPR. ISO/IEC 27001, by contrast, is a certifiable standard rather than a regulation. Certification runs on a three-year cycle, with surveillance audits in the intervening years and a full recertification audit at the end. Failing an ISO audit costs you a certificate; failing a regulator costs you a fine.

Building block technologies

Study guide: Ch 1 › Virtualization

Cloud computing rests on a set of foundational technologies. Virtualization abstracts physical hardware so that multiple isolated operating system instances can run on a single physical server, and it underpins most infrastructure as a service offerings. Storage, networking, databases, and orchestration are the other building blocks that, together with virtualization, allow a provider to deliver flexible, scalable services.

Example

A single physical server in a data centre might run a hypervisor that hosts a dozen separate virtual machines, each belonging to a different customer, each believing it has its own dedicated server. That is virtualization at work.

A closer look at each building block

Several of these building blocks carry additional detail worth knowing.

  • Virtualization and tenancy, customers sharing a provider's pooled hardware are called tenants, like individuals renting space in an office building, and each tenant uses the environment without any knowledge of the others. Security cautionThe hypervisor controls every virtual machine on a host, so a compromised hypervisor exposes everything above it. Securing the hypervisor is the provider's responsibility, and it is one of the strongest arguments for scrutinising a provider before you commit.
  • Storage, storage area networks (SANs) and network attached storage (NAS) underpin the flexible storage that providers offer. When a disk is deallocated from one customer and reallocated to another, file fragments can remain, and the customer has no way to securely wipe drives they do not physically control. In practiceCrypto-shredding, destroying the encryption keys so any remaining fragments are unreadable, is the practical answer, which is one reason to encrypt cloud data from the start.
  • Networking, cloud resources are reached over untrusted networks, so traffic transmitted in the clear can be intercepted and read in a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack. Encryption and secure transport protect data in motion. In practiceThe working rule for personal data heading to the cloud: encrypt in all three states, at rest, in transit, and in use, and remember that encryption is named explicitly in the text of the GDPR as an appropriate technical safeguard.
  • Databases, when a database is consumed as a cloud service, administration of the underlying database, including patching and tuning, becomes the provider's responsibility. Data warehouses, data lakes, and non-relational stores are available alongside traditional relational databases. In practiceSome providers deliver databases as a PaaS offering, which is exactly why the administration sits with the provider in that model.
  • Orchestration, like a conductor keeping an orchestra together, orchestration tooling keeps data, processes, and application services synchronised across multiple clouds and SaaS subscriptions, presenting one seemingly seamless environment to the user. It is more than automating a task here and there; automation is the mechanism, orchestration is the outcome. Trade-offVendor orchestration tools such as AWS CloudFormation vary considerably in capability and, crucially, in how well they integrate with other vendors' clouds, so evaluate the integration story before committing.

Service categories, deployment models, and shared considerations

Cloud service capability types

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Computing Service Categories

Before the familiar service categories, the reference architecture describes three capability types. These describe what the customer is actually able to control, and they map directly onto the service categories, so it is worth learning them as a pair rather than separately.

  • Infrastructure capability type, the customer can provision and control processing, storage, and networking resources. This corresponds to IaaS.
  • Platform capability type, the customer can deploy and control its own applications using programming languages, libraries, and tools that the provider supports, without managing the infrastructure underneath. This corresponds to PaaS.
  • Application capability type, the customer can use the provider's applications but does not control the underlying platform or infrastructure. This corresponds to SaaS.
Example

Think of it as three answers to the question "what do I get to control?" With infrastructure capability you control the machine. With platform capability you control the code. With application capability you control only your data and settings within someone else's finished product.

Cloud computing activities

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Computing Reference Architecture

The reference architecture also sets out the activities each party performs. In broad terms the cloud service customer performs activities such as using the service, administering its own users, and monitoring its own consumption. The cloud service provider performs activities such as operating the service, managing the infrastructure, and providing audit data. The cloud service partner performs supporting activities such as auditing, brokering, or developing on top of the service. Knowing who performs which activity is what makes the shared responsibility model workable in practice.

Example

When a maritime company's cloud platform needs a security patch applied to the underlying operating system in a PaaS arrangement, that is a provider activity. When the same company decides which of its staff should have administrative rights in the application, that is a customer activity. Neither party can perform the other's activity, which is why the boundary must be understood before an incident, not during one.

Within the partner grouping, three specific actors are named often enough to be worth learning individually. They come from the NIST cloud reference architecture and appear in most CCSP courseware.

  • Cloud auditor, an independent party that examines a provider's controls and issues an objective assessment. The auditor's remit is broader than security alone, covering performance and privacy impact as well.
  • Cloud broker, an intermediary that manages the use, performance, and delivery of cloud services, and negotiates relationships between provider and customer. Brokers typically add value through service intermediation, aggregation, or arbitrage.
  • Cloud carrier, the party providing the connectivity and transport that carries the service between provider and customer. In practice this is usually a network or telecommunications operator.
Example

A telecom company subscribes to a SaaS platform. The SaaS vendor is the provider, the telecom company is the customer, the audit firm engaged to review the vendor's controls is the cloud auditor, and the network operator carrying the traffic between them is the cloud carrier. All four are distinct actors with distinct responsibilities, even though only two of them are party to the subscription contract.

How consumption is billed in each category

The billing dimensions each category typically uses follow directly from what the consumer controls. SaaS consumers are commonly billed by the number of end users, time of use, network bandwidth consumed, or the amount and duration of data stored. PaaS consumers are billed according to the processing, database, storage, and network resources their application consumes, plus the duration of platform usage. IaaS consumers are billed on measures such as CPU hours used by virtual machines, the volume and duration of data stored, bandwidth consumed, and even the number of IP addresses held for a given period. If a scenario question describes billing by named user, think SaaS; billing by CPU hour points to IaaS.

One IaaS-adjacent service worth naming here is the content delivery network (CDN): a system of distributed servers that delivers web content to users based on their geographical location, the origin of the page, and the point of presence closest to the user. CDNs exist to improve the speed and performance of content delivery, and they reappear later in the course in the context of availability.

Cloud service categories

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Computing Service Categories

The three familiar service categories describe how much of the technology stack the provider manages, and how much remains the customer's responsibility.

  • Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), where the provider manages the physical infrastructure and virtualization layer, and the customer manages the operating system, middleware, and applications.
  • Platform as a Service (PaaS), where the provider also manages the operating system and runtime environment, and the customer focuses on deploying its own application code using supported languages and tools.
  • Software as a Service (SaaS), where the provider manages nearly the entire stack, and the customer simply uses the application, typically configuring settings and managing its own data and user access. In practiceSaaS reaches customers in two delivery modes: hosted application management, where commercially available software is hosted and delivered over the web, and software on demand, network access to a single application copy built specifically for SaaS delivery.
Example

A bank running its own custom trading application on virtual machines it manages, using a provider only for the underlying compute and storage, is using IaaS. If that same bank instead built the application using a provider's managed application platform, without worrying about the underlying servers, that would be PaaS. If the bank instead simply subscribed to a commercial off the shelf trading platform delivered entirely over the browser, that would be SaaS.

Cloud deployment models

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Deployment Models
  • Public cloud, infrastructure provisioned for open use by the general public, typically owned and operated by the provider.
  • Private cloud, infrastructure provisioned for exclusive use by a single organization, offering greater control at typically higher cost.
  • Community cloud, infrastructure provisioned for exclusive use by a specific group of organizations that share common concerns, such as compliance requirements or mission objectives.
  • Hybrid cloud, a composition of two or more distinct cloud infrastructures, such as private and public, that remain separate entities but are bound together by technology enabling data and application portability between them. In practiceHybrid designs enable cloud bursting, where workloads overflow from private capacity into the public cloud during demand peaks, and hybrid storage patterns that replicate local data to a public cloud for archive and backup.
  • Multi-cloud, the use of multiple public cloud providers, often to avoid dependence on a single vendor or to take advantage of different providers' strengths.
Example

A group of port authorities in the same region might jointly fund and use a shared logistics platform built specifically to serve their common regulatory and operational needs. That arrangement is a community cloud, since it serves a defined group with shared concerns rather than the public at large or a single organization alone.

Community cloud is usually the least familiar of the five models in day-to-day work, so real deployments are worth naming, along with a model that shows why the arrangement holds together.

Examples from the field

Real community clouds cluster around shared regulation. Government and public sector: AWS GovCloud for US government workloads, and the UK's G-Cloud framework through which public sector bodies procure cloud services. Health: shared platforms serving groups of hospital trusts, built around patient data handling standards and interoperability needs. Finance: groups of banks sharing infrastructure built to meet PCI DSS. Energy: grid operators and utilities sharing environments built around critical infrastructure protection standards. Education: Janet, the shared network through which UK education and research institutions pool resources, research data collaboration, and compliance around student data protection.

Worked example

Consider police forces covering London, Berkshire, and Buckinghamshire. All three follow the same structure, hold the same kinds of sensitive data, face the same security requirements, and work to constrained budgets. A community cloud lets them share physical infrastructure while keeping each force's data logically segregated, and all three receive the same security patches and updates. The residual challenges are operational rather than architectural: agreeing the pricing model and coordinating when patches are applied.

Multi-cloud earns a note of its own, because its rationale differs from the other four models.

Why multi-cloud helps availability

The core argument for multi-cloud is availability, the A of the CIA triad: it is very unlikely that two distinct providers in different locations suffer an incident simultaneously, so spreading workloads across providers protects against a single provider's outage. A major provider disruption in 2025 rippled into critical national infrastructure, including hours of disruption to a major UK airport's departure operations, an illustration of how far a single provider's failure can reach. Multi-cloud also lets you place workloads in regions close to your users, patients, or constituents to reduce latency.

Cloud shared considerations

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Shared Considerations

These are cross cutting concerns that apply across every service category and deployment model, and the exam frequently tests whether you can correctly identify which consideration a scenario is describing.

  • Interoperability, the ability of different systems to work together and exchange information without extensive custom integration.
  • Portability, the ability to move applications and data between providers or environments without being unreasonably constrained by proprietary formats.
  • Reversibility, the ability to retrieve data and cease use of a service, including having that data verifiably removed from the provider's environment.
  • Availability, resiliency, and performance, covering whether the service is accessible and performs as expected, including under failure conditions.
  • Security, privacy, and governance, covering the protection of data and systems, and the policies that guide decision making.
  • Auditability and regulatory considerations, covering the ability to review controls and evidence, and to meet applicable legal obligations.
  • Service levels and SLAs, the contractual commitments a provider makes regarding availability, performance, and support.
  • Maintenance and versioning, who is responsible for upgrades and patching, and how software versions are tracked and communicated.
  • Outsourcing, the implications of handing operational responsibility to a third party, including the governance needed to retain oversight.

Two of these deserve a closer look, because they are easy to skim past and both appear in the exam outline. Maintenance and versioning shifts with the service category. Under SaaS the provider handles upgrades, patching, and maintenance entirely. Under PaaS and IaaS some of that responsibility moves back to the customer, so the contract and SLA need to state plainly who does what, and version numbers need to be recorded whenever a platform or application is upgraded. Outsourcing recognises that using a cloud service means handing operational work to someone else. The organisation gains a specialist team and elastic architecture, but it does not hand over accountability, which is why governance and oversight remain firmly with the customer.

Example

A bank on SaaS payroll finds the vendor has upgraded the platform overnight, changing a report format the bank's finance team depends on. Nothing was breached and the vendor acted within its rights, but the bank had not negotiated notice of version changes. That is a maintenance and versioning failure in the contract, not a security incident.

Reversibility in practice

A UK bank negotiating a cloud contract insists on a clause guaranteeing that if it ends the relationship, it can retrieve its data in a usable format and have the provider certify that all copies have been deleted within a set number of days. That requirement is reversibility. It is a different concern from interoperability, which is about systems working together while the relationship continues, not about exiting it.

Service levels in practice

An SLA is not only about security. Minimum uptime commitments are common, with some customers paying a premium for the five nines, 99.999 percent availability, and support responsiveness is contractual too. If a provider fails to meet the commitments delineated in the contract, the customer is typically entitled to financial remedies such as service credits, and persistent failure to rectify performance can become grounds for termination. SLAs should also address versioning: whenever a platform is upgraded, version numbers should be recorded and an overlap period agreed during which the previous version remains available, so a customer is not stranded by an overnight change.

Example from the field

At Rackspace, the managed security business worked to a three-hour SLA to notify a customer if a threat actor was spotted in their environment. The team went further than the contract required: around the time the GDPR came into force they downloaded the ICO's personal data breach notification form, freely available at ico.org.uk, and used the three hours to collate who and what had been accessed into that form, so the customer received a report already shaped for the supervisory authority. Under the GDPR a controller has 72 hours from becoming aware of a breach to notify the ICO, and that clock includes weekends: a breach discovered late on Friday must be reported by Monday evening.

Data sovereignty and your contracts

A standing recommendation: take your cloud contracts to your GRC team and check what assurance you actually have about data location. Personal data of European citizens should stay within the European Economic Area or, failing that, within countries holding an adequacy decision; the UK is itself an adequacy country, and the ICO website maintains the current list. The stakes are statutory: the GDPR's upper tier of fines reaches 20 million euros or 4 percent of global annual turnover from the previous year, whichever is higher. The contract's responsibility matrix will also show, without ambiguity, which obligations sit with the provider and which with you.

Example from practice

One organisation that carried out exactly this review discovered that personal data of European citizens was being stored in a data centre region in China. Nothing in the day-to-day operation of the service had made that visible; only the contract review surfaced it. That is the kind of finding that turns a routine review into a compliance programme.

In practice

Most organisations run a mix of everything in this section at once, which makes governance very dynamic, and SLAs are long documents that need legal expertise to dissect. That points to a recurring theme of cloud security: the enemy is not primarily the nation state or the hacking group. The enemy is complexity. Every broker, layer, and integration you add is another place for a control to silently fail.

Impact of related technologies

Study guide: Ch 1 › Emerging Technologies

The cloud reference architecture also considers how emerging technologies influence design decisions, including artificial intelligence, machine learning, blockchain, the Internet of Things, containers, quantum computing, edge computing, and confidential computing. A cloud architect needs to understand not just the traditional building blocks, but also how these newer technologies change the risk picture and the design choices available.

Example

An energy company considering edge computing for its remote monitoring sensors has to weigh the benefit of processing data closer to the source, which reduces latency, against the added complexity of securing many small, distributed compute nodes rather than a single centralized data centre.

Several of these technologies carry practical detail worth keeping. The key points are below.

  • Containers, a wrapper packaging code, configuration, and libraries into a single deployable unit, so there is no need to confirm that a large number of separate files deployed and synchronised correctly across every system serving the application. This maps directly to the I of the CIA triad: containers raise integrity while cutting deployment time and cost.
  • Quantum computing, most public key cryptography rests on prime number factorisation being computationally impractical; a sufficiently capable quantum computer breaks that assumption, so the field matters to anyone whose architecture leans on cryptography, and quantum key distribution is already appearing in security curricula. In practiceIn a recent address given from Bletchley Park, senior UK national security leaders admitted openly that they cannot predict what the technology landscape will look like in five to ten years, given AI, machine learning, IoT, and quantum computing together. Humility about the horizon is now the official position.
  • Edge computing, brings compute physically closer to users, cutting latency, increasing downstream bandwidth and reducing upstream bandwidth. Applications include augmented and virtual reality, 360-degree video, cloud gaming, and ad insertion. Edge and cloud are complementary rather than competing: capture the right data at the edge, then use the storage and processing power of the cloud behind it, and start with small pilot projects.
  • Confidential computing, uses secure enclave technology to create a trusted execution environment (TEE), built on security features provided by CPU vendors, allowing encryption, decryption, and isolation inside the processor's memory. It closes a long-standing gap: encryption at rest and in transit are solved problems, but data has always had to be decrypted in memory to be processed, which is exactly where confidential computing now protects it. Common questionDo cloud providers deliver confidential computing by default? Many do, but they are not all created equal, and it remains an emerging capability. Check whether your contract references protection of data in use before assuming it.
  • Blockchain, a cryptographically linked list of records in which each new block carries the timestamp, metadata, and cryptographic hash of the previous one. Its security contribution is non-repudiation, the property that the origin of a piece of data cannot be disputed, best treated as a companion to the CIA triad rather than part of it.
  • Internet of Things (IoT), devices with enormous automation value and, very often, no security designed in at all. Example from the fieldSecurity researchers have bought second-hand smart scales from online marketplaces and reverse engineered them, recovering previous owners' names, home addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, and even health information. Corporate policy on bringing IoT devices into the workplace, and on disposing of them, deserves real attention.
  • Machine learning and AI, beyond security tooling, machine learning drives cloud scaling itself: rather than relying on pre-programmed utilisation thresholds, adaptive systems take a broader view of demand and adjust resources in real time, keeping consumption tightly matched to demand.
1.3 Security concepts relevant to cloud computing Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 1, Architectural Concepts, and Chapter 4, Security in the Cloud (Virtualization and Shared Cloud Platform Risks sections)

Foundational security concepts every CCSP candidate needs

Cryptography and key management

Study guide: Ch 3 › Designing and Applying Security Strategies for Storage (Encryption; Certificate Management)

Encryption protects data by transforming it into a form that cannot be read without the correct key. In the cloud, key management is just as important as the encryption itself. A well encrypted dataset offers little protection if the key sits alongside it, unmanaged and unrotated.

Good practice

A bank stores encryption keys in a dedicated key management service or hardware security module, separate from the encrypted data itself, and rotates those keys on a defined schedule. If the data store is ever compromised, the attacker still cannot read the data without separately compromising the key management system.

The key management lifecycle

Key management is best understood as a lifecycle with five stages, each with its own failure modes. Generation: keys must be created with high entropy using algorithms that meet established standards, NIST recommendations being the default reference, and ideally generated inside a hardware security module (HSM) so the process itself is shielded. Distribution: the most exposed stage, since mishandling invites interception; use encrypted channels, wrap keys in a further layer of encryption while in transit, and verify the recipient's identity before release. Usage: enforce least privilege on who and what can use a key, and in regulated environments keep audit trails recording when, where, and by whom keys were used, so anomalous use is detectable. Storage: keys at rest must themselves be encrypted and always managed separately from the data they encrypt; a key stored beside its ciphertext protects nothing. Destruction: retire keys at end of life using cryptographic erasure, and where a third party performs destruction, obtain a certificate of destruction as compliance evidence.

Example from the field

RSA hardware tokens, the key fobs showing a six-digit code that changed every sixty seconds, each fob associated with a named employee, were for years the industry's standard way of binding an authentication secret to a person. The technology has moved on, but the discipline has not: an authentication secret is only as strong as the process that binds it to a person and manages it through its life.

Where the key management system lives

  • On-premises KMS, complete control over the full key lifecycle, hosted in your own data centre; the right fit for the most stringent regulatory requirements. Trade-offHigh cost for hardware and infrastructure, and it demands scarce in-house expertise to run properly.
  • Cloud-based KMS, provided by the CSP and already integrated with its platform; highly available, scalable, pay as you use, with little upfront investment. Trade-offYou are trusting the provider with your keys, and key security becomes a joint effort under the shared responsibility model.
  • Hybrid KMS, the most sensitive keys stay on premises while the cloud handles less sensitive operations. Trade-offMore complex to implement and operate, so it needs careful planning to make the two halves work as one.

Encryption across the three states of data

For data at rest, AES is the standard symmetric choice, with RSA commonly involved in key management. For data in transit, TLS encrypts application traffic, and where TLS is not suitable, IPsec can encrypt the entire packet, which is why it is the usual choice for VPN connections. End-to-end encryption goes further still: data is encrypted at the source and decrypted only by the intended recipient, with no intermediate decryption points, implemented with public key infrastructure, where the sender encrypts with the recipient's public key and only the matching private key can decrypt. Its main operational challenge is exactly the key distribution problem described above, multiplied by the number of endpoints.

Watch this in the exam

End-to-end encryption may appear abbreviated as E2EE. Recognise the abbreviation, and remember what makes it distinct: no intermediate decryption points, not merely encryption on each hop.

Identity and access control

Study guide: Ch 6 › Identity and Access Management; Ch 5 › Identity Assurance in Cloud and Virtual Environments

Cloud environments distinguish between different types of access. User access covers individual human accounts. Privilege access covers elevated permissions typically reserved for administrators. Service access covers the permissions granted to automated services and applications, such as one system calling another through an API, which should generally be scoped as narrowly as possible.

Example

A telecom company gives its billing application a service account with permission to read customer records from a database, but no permission to delete them. If that application is ever compromised, the damage an attacker can do through it is limited by the narrow scope of that service account.

Example from the field

The 2025 attacks on major UK retailers, including Marks and Spencer and the Co-op, began with social engineering that yielded legitimate administrative credentials, which is what made them so damaging: the attackers did not break the perimeter, they walked through it as trusted users. Privileged accounts, service accounts, root, and administrator access are notoriously difficult to monitor for misuse precisely because their activity looks authorised. Detection depends on the accumulation of weak signals along the kill chain, an unusual privilege escalation here, compression tools appearing in a sensitive area there, each individually explainable, together malicious.

Data and media sanitization

Study guide: Ch 2 › Data Control › Data Destruction/Disposal

When storage is decommissioned or reassigned, any data it held must be rendered unrecoverable. Overwriting replaces the original data with new patterns of bits. Cryptographic erase instead destroys the encryption key that protected the data, which makes the data permanently unreadable even though the underlying bits remain on the disk. Cryptographic erase is particularly useful in cloud environments, where physical destruction of shared media is often impossible.

Example

An energy company decommissions a virtual disk that once held sensitive engineering data. Because the disk was encrypted from the start, the company can simply destroy the encryption key. The underlying storage blocks can then be safely reassigned to another tenant, since the data on them is now permanently unreadable.

This is harder in cloud than the summary above suggests. Cloud data is regularly relocated and backed up as the provider optimises its estate, so there is no practical way to confirm that every copy of a data set has been removed, and no fixed time frame after which lingering fragments can be assumed gone. The physical medium is out of your reach entirely. Two practices follow. First, encrypt sensitive data from the moment it enters the cloud, so cryptographic erase remains available at end of life. Second, where physical media does come back under your control, a specialist third party can degauss, shred, or incinerate it and issue a certificate of destruction, which is the evidence a regulator or auditor will ask for.

Network security

Study guide: Ch 5 › Securing Communications and Infrastructure

Network security groups filter traffic based on attributes such as source, destination, port, and protocol. Traffic inspection examines the content or behaviour of network traffic, including internal traffic between microservices, to detect malicious activity. Geofencing restricts or permits access based on the geographic origin of a request, which is often used to support data residency requirements.

Example

A maritime company operating in UK waters configures geofencing so that administrative access to its vessel tracking platform is only permitted from IP addresses within the UK and a small number of approved partner countries. A login attempt from an unexpected country is automatically blocked, regardless of whether the correct password was used.

Two further ideas sit alongside these and appear throughout cloud security material. Zero trust abandons the assumption that anything inside the network perimeter is inherently trustworthy. Every request is authenticated and authorised on its own merits, regardless of where it comes from. Segmentation and micro-segmentation divide a network into separate security zones, micro-segmentation going as far as the individual workload, so that an attacker who compromises one component cannot move laterally to reach others.

Example

Under a traditional perimeter model, a server that had already been reached from inside the corporate network would be trusted by default. Under zero trust, that same request still has to prove who it is and that it is entitled to the specific resource it is asking for. If an attacker gets a foothold on one machine, micro-segmentation then limits how far that foothold can take them.

Several further details sharpen this picture. Network security groups apply at more than one scope: rules can attach at the virtual machine, the network interface, or the subnet level, acting as a virtual firewall at each. Traffic direction has its own vocabulary: north-south for traffic entering and leaving the environment, east-west for lateral movement inside it. It is the east-west traffic where most damage is done once an attacker has a foothold, which is what segmentation and micro-segmentation exist to restrict. Geofencing feeds into detections such as impossible travel: for example, an account logging in from Madeira and then from London fifteen minutes later, which no legitimate user can do. And zero trust compresses to a strapline worth memorising: never trust, always verify, for every request, regardless of origin, even from a segment that has already authenticated once. Unified threat management (UTM) appliances also belong in this picture: single solutions combining firewall, VPN, antivirus, and intrusion detection functions to protect both virtual and physical networks, with the bottleneck caution below applying doubly to them.

In practice

A former head of the CIA put the monitoring posture plainly: it is not if but when an organisation is successfully targeted, and the most effective posture is to assume compromise. A related caution: next generation firewall is more marketing term than category, and every extra function loaded onto a device sitting at a bottleneck in the network adds latency at that choke point. Weigh the functionality against the position it occupies.

Virtualization security

Study guide: Ch 1 › Virtualization Security; Ch 5 › Managing Virtual Systems

Multiple customers often share the same underlying physical hardware, separated only by logical boundaries enforced by a hypervisor or container runtime. A vulnerability that allows one virtual machine to break out of its isolation boundary and access another tenant's resources is known as a VM escape, or hypervisor breakout. This is one of the more serious threats specific to virtualized, multi-tenant cloud environments. Ephemeral computing and serverless technology introduce their own isolation considerations, since compute resources may be extremely short lived and shared rapidly across many different workloads.

Example

A security researcher discovers a flaw in a hypervisor that allows carefully crafted instructions from within one virtual machine to execute code on the host itself, potentially reaching other virtual machines on the same physical server. This is exactly the kind of isolation failure that keeps cloud infrastructure teams focused on hypervisor patching and hardening.

Three further points are worth noting. First, powered-off virtual machines are not safe the way powered-off physical servers are: a physical server that is off is isolated from attack, but a VM exists as an image file in storage, so if the hypervisor or storage layer is compromised, images can be attacked, corrupted, or tampered with even while the machine is disabled. Second, virtual switches share the hosting environment with the workloads they connect, so a compromised host puts the virtual switching layer within reach as well. Third, hypervisors cap the resources any one guest can consume, which is the built-in defence against one tenant mounting a denial of service against the others; remember that a DoS can originate inside the cloud from a co-tenant, not only from the internet.

Virtualization security is wider than hypervisors alone. Containers offer a lightweight alternative to full virtual machines, isolating applications without a guest operating system; securing them means controlling access rights, hardening the orchestration platform such as Kubernetes, and building only from trusted images. Ephemeral computing takes a different route to safety: resources exist only while they are needed, so the window of opportunity for an attacker shrinks with the lifespan of the resource. Serverless computing abstracts the server layer away entirely, shifting more of the security responsibility onto the provider and leaving the customer to concentrate on securing the code and its data inputs and outputs. Isolation between tenants sharing serverless or container execution environments remains a live concern, and a tested one in this domain.

Common question

Why does virtualization security feature in the exam at all, given that under the shared responsibility model it belongs to the provider and the consumer never touches it? The responsibility split is real, but it still matters to you for two reasons. The CCSP expects you to understand the mechanisms on the provider's side of the fence, because you are the one accepting the residual risk. And you are not powerless: your security operations centre can monitor for the symptoms, and your contract review should confirm the provider is doing what it claims, especially where regulated data is involved.

Common cloud threats

Study guide: Ch 4 › Shared Cloud Platform Risks and Responsibilities; Ch 6 › Common Threats to Applications

Certain threats appear repeatedly in cloud environments and are worth knowing by name. Account or credential compromise involves an attacker gaining access using stolen or misused credentials, often exploiting weak identity and access management practices. Insecure APIs can expose functionality or data beyond what was intended. Data remanence is the risk that data persists on storage media after it was supposed to be deleted, which is why proper sanitization matters. Shared technology vulnerabilities arise from the multi-tenant nature of cloud infrastructure itself.

Example

An attacker obtains a developer's cloud console credentials through a phishing email, then uses that access to create new virtual machines for cryptocurrency mining at the victim organization's expense. This is a straightforward example of account compromise, and it illustrates why strong authentication and monitoring for unusual account activity both matter.

Security hygiene

Study guide: Ch 5 › OS Hardening, Monitoring, and Remediation

Good cloud security depends on consistent, ongoing practices rather than one time fixes. Patching addresses known vulnerabilities as they are discovered. Baselining establishes a known, secure reference configuration against which future changes can be measured. Immutable architecture avoids modifying running infrastructure in place, instead deploying a new, pre-configured instance for any change and retiring the old one. Hardening reduces a system's attack surface by disabling unnecessary services and features.

Example

Rather than logging into a running web server to apply a security patch, a bank's cloud team builds a new server image with the patch already applied, tests it, and then replaces the running instances with the new image. The old instances are simply terminated. This immutable approach avoids the configuration drift that can build up over years of incremental, in-place changes.

Patching and incident response both deserve more depth. On patching, industry figures put the typical time to apply fixes at 100 to 120 days, a window in which known vulnerabilities sit exposed, and many organisations settle for a monthly cycle as the achievable compromise. Keeping a feed of emerging vulnerabilities helps prioritisation; a weekly aggregated digest from a service such as VirusTotal works well. On resilience, segment your backups so a ransomware campaign that reaches production cannot also reach the copies you would restore from. Periodic SWOT analysis of the defensive architecture, plus penetration testing, round out the hygiene picture: black box tests simulate an outside attacker who must do their own reconnaissance, while white box tests give the testers material such as access control lists, topology diagrams, and data flow diagrams, trading realism for lower cost and shorter duration. A counter-trend is worth knowing: as digital defences improve, attackers are reverting to traditional methods such as dumpster diving for discarded personal data.

Why incident response plans fail

Many incident response plans run to a hundred steps and fall at step one: contact a named individual in IT security, by email. One person is a single point of failure, and one channel that depends on the very infrastructure under attack is not a channel at all; if the incident has taken out your ISP or mail platform, the plan is unreachable. Effective plans name backups for every role, use out-of-band communications that can ride cellular networks as well as corporate Wi-Fi, and delineate accountability so precisely that no step can be met with a shrug. In a live incident, everyone should already know where they stand and what they own.

Why this matters for the exam

  • Distinguish overwriting from cryptographic erase. Cryptographic erase is generally the more practical choice in shared cloud storage, since the customer often cannot physically access or destroy the underlying media.
  • Distinguish network security groups, which filter based on traffic attributes, from geofencing, which filters based on geography. They solve different problems even though both control access.
  • Recognise VM escape scenarios. Questions describing an attacker moving from one tenant's resources to another's, without any credential theft involved, are usually pointing at a virtualization or isolation failure rather than an access control problem.

Building security into cloud architecture from the start

The cloud secure data lifecycle

Study guide: Ch 3 › Cloud Data Lifecycle

Data moves through a defined set of stages from the moment it is created until it is finally destroyed. Understanding this lifecycle helps a security professional apply the right controls at the right time, rather than treating data protection as a single, one time activity.

  • Create, the point at which data is generated or acquired.
  • Store, committing the data to a storage repository.
  • Use, data being viewed, processed, or otherwise used by an application or person.
  • Share, data being made accessible to other users, systems, or organizations.
  • Archive, data leaving active use and being retained for a defined period, often for legal or business reasons. In practicePersonal data must never be retained indefinitely; retention periods are typically tied to the underlying contract, commonly 12 or 24 months.
  • Destroy, the permanent removal of data using secure sanitization methods. In practicePhysical destruction options include degaussing, shredding, crushing, and incineration, with a certificate of destruction issued by the third party performing it.
Example

A maritime company collects sensor data from a vessel, stores it in a cloud data lake, uses it to generate a fuel efficiency report, shares a summary with a partner shipping line, archives the raw data for two years to satisfy a regulatory requirement, and then securely destroys it once that retention period expires. That is the full lifecycle in practice.

Business continuity and disaster recovery planning

Study guide: Ch 4 › Disaster Recovery (DR) and Business Continuity (BC); Ch 8 › Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

Cloud based business continuity and disaster recovery planning ensures an organization can keep operating, or recover quickly, when something goes wrong. Two metrics anchor this planning. Recovery Time Objective, or RTO, is the maximum acceptable time to restore a service after an outage. Recovery Point Objective, or RPO, is the maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured as a period of time. These two figures are easy to confuse, but they answer different questions: RTO asks how long you can be down, and RPO asks how much data you can afford to lose.

Example

An energy company sets an RTO of one hour and an RPO of fifteen minutes for a critical monitoring system. This means the business has decided it can tolerate being offline for up to an hour after a failure, but it can only afford to lose the most recent fifteen minutes of data. The recovery strategy, including how frequently backups are taken, must be designed to meet both figures.

Examples from the field

Interruptions are not only cyber attacks, and recent years supply plenty of reminders. Wildfires have threatened data centre operations in more than one region. Flooding has reached the Middle East, where cloud seeding operations have produced floods around facilities built partly underground for cooling, which is exactly where water does the most damage. And the large-scale power blackout across parts of southern Europe in 2025 showed how a grid event becomes an IT event: uninterruptible power supplies bridge the first minutes on battery while longer-term generators start up, which is why the UPS estate is one of the components a business impact analysis should rank as most critical. Insurers group these under acts of God, but the continuity plan has to treat them as ordinary inputs.

Business Impact Analysis

Study guide: Ch 1 › Business Requirements › Cost/Benefit Analysis; Ch 4 › Cloud-Specific BIA Concerns

A Business Impact Analysis identifies which business functions are critical, assesses the impact if those functions are disrupted, and from that analysis derives recovery priorities such as RTO and RPO. A cost-benefit analysis is often used alongside this work, comparing the cost of implementing a given level of resilience against the financial and operational benefit of doing so. Return on investment is a related financial metric, often calculated as part of that broader cost-benefit comparison.

Example

Before migrating its billing platform to the cloud, a telecom operator first identifies that billing is a critical function, since an outage would directly stop revenue collection. It then assesses the financial impact of an hour of downtime versus a day of downtime, and uses that analysis to justify the additional cost of a more resilient, multi-region deployment.

In practice

Run the threat identification part of a BIA as a cross-functional exercise, not a security-team brainstorm. We do not see the world as it is; we see it as we are. People from different business units surface failure modes that security professionals miss, because they live with different dependencies.

Impact is one half of the analysis; cost is the other. The cost-benefit case behind a cloud decision draws on nine recurring factors: resource pooling, the shift from capital to operational expenditure, time and efficiency gains, depreciation (technology loses value the moment it is bought, like a car driven off the forecourt, which favours leasing capacity over owning it), reduced maintenance and configuration effort, a shift in staff focus away from daily firefighting, lower utilities costs for power and cooling, licensing paid for what is used rather than bought in bulk, and pay-per-usage billing that can be charged back to the departments consuming it. The outcome is usually judged as cloud return on investment, and the trade is worth stating plainly: moving to a public cloud provider reduces upfront cost but increases monthly expense, so a positive ROI depends on the savings and efficiencies outweighing that recurring spend.

Functional security requirements

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Shared Considerations › Outsourcing Issues

Several requirements recur throughout cloud architecture discussions, and the exam expects you to distinguish between them precisely.

  • Portability, the ability to move applications and data between providers without being unreasonably constrained by proprietary formats.
  • Interoperability, the ability of different systems to exchange and use information without extensive custom integration.
  • Vendor lock-in, the risk of becoming so dependent on a provider's proprietary technology that switching becomes difficult or impractical.
Example

A bank builds heavy customization on top of a SaaS provider's proprietary workflow engine. Two years later, the bank wants to switch providers, but the cost of rebuilding those customizations elsewhere is so high that switching is effectively not a realistic option. That is vendor lock-in, and it is a direct consequence of insufficient attention to portability during the original design.

All three terms carry more operational depth than the definitions suggest. Portability splits into a one-time movement, where a customer moves to a platform with no intention of moving again, and the now more common repeated movement between providers. Neither is a forklift operation: each CSP uses different tools and templates, so a move needs careful mapping, and data can be lost or modified in the process; if that data is personal data, the loss itself is a regulatory liability under the GDPR. There is also a commercial squeeze: you keep paying for the old service while porting to the new one, which puts real time pressure on the migration. On lock-in, a concrete example is AWS CloudTrail: it gives strong audit capability for governance, risk, and compliance, but a decision to leave AWS means rebuilding that GRC functionality from scratch on whatever replaces it. Well-designed APIs mitigate interoperability gaps, for instance letting a SaaS data inventory feed classification information into GRC tooling, so there is a single source of truth rather than divergent classifications in different systems.

In practice

Infrastructure as code helps here: if the way workloads are spun up is captured in Terraform, the definitions are reusable across environments, which softens both the portability problem and the lock-in risk. Infrastructure as code is covered in more depth in a later domain.

Security responsibilities across service categories

Study guide: Ch 5 › Foundations of Managed Services › Shared Responsibilities by Service Type

As an organization moves from IaaS toward SaaS, the provider takes on an increasing share of security responsibility. In IaaS, the customer typically manages the operating system, middleware, and application layers. In PaaS, the provider manages the operating system and runtime, and the customer focuses on the application. In SaaS, the provider manages nearly the entire stack, and the customer's responsibility narrows mainly to managing its own data and user access.

Example

A bank running its own virtual machines on IaaS is responsible for patching the operating system itself. If that same bank instead uses a SaaS platform for the same function, the provider is responsible for patching the operating system, since the bank has no access to or control over that layer.

Watch this in the exam

Whatever the service category, one asset is always the customer's responsibility: the data, along with the APIs and applications the customer exposes. A useful IaaS heuristic: everything above the hypervisor is the customer's. But the boundary is a continuum that varies between providers and contracts, so in any real engagement it must be established case by case, from the responsibility matrix in the contract, not assumed from the category name.

Cloud design patterns

Study guide: Ch 4 › Cloud Design Patterns

Several established frameworks help architects design secure, resilient cloud systems rather than starting from scratch each time. The Well-Architected Framework organizes design decisions around pillars such as operational excellence, security, reliability, performance efficiency, and cost optimization. The Cloud Security Alliance Enterprise Architecture provides a broader, methodology driven approach to cloud security architecture. Secure by design means building security into a system from the earliest stages, rather than adding it afterward. The SANS security principles are also referenced as a design pattern in the exam outline.

The SANS security principles are a set of cloud security expectations spanning the whole service, rather than a single technical control. They cover protection of data in transit, asset protection and resilience, separation between customers, a governance framework, operational security, personnel security, secure development, supply chain security, secure user management, identity and authentication, external interface protection, secure service administration, audit information and alerting for customers, and secure use of the service. You do not need to recite the list, but you should recognise that they span people, process, and technology, and that several of them place obligations on the customer rather than the provider.

The CSA Enterprise Architecture is a methodology and reference model for building a cloud security programme. It is organised into layered domains, including Business Operation Support Services (BOSS), Information Technology Operation and Support (ITOS), presentation, application, information, and infrastructure services, with Security and Risk Management running across them. It deliberately draws on established frameworks rather than replacing them: SABSA for business-driven security architecture, ITIL for service management practice, TOGAF for enterprise architecture method, and the Jericho Forum principles for de-perimeterisation, which hold that security must be built into data and applications because the network boundary can no longer be relied upon.

Example

An architect using the CSA Enterprise Architecture to plan a cloud security programme would work down the layers, deciding what is needed at the business support level, the operations level, and the infrastructure level, rather than starting with a list of products to buy. The value is in the structure it imposes on the conversation.

Watch this in the exam

Keep design patterns separate from control frameworks. The design patterns named in this subdomain are the SANS security principles, the Well-Architected Framework, the CSA Enterprise Architecture, and secure by design. The CSA Cloud Controls Matrix is not a design pattern. It is a catalogue of controls used to assess providers, and it belongs with CSP evaluation and the STAR registry.

Example

A maritime company applies the Well-Architected Framework when designing its new hybrid cloud platform, explicitly weighing decisions such as choosing a more expensive but more reliable storage tier against the framework's cost optimization and reliability pillars, rather than defaulting to the cheapest option without considering the trade-off.

Returning to the SANS security principles listed earlier, several deserve practical detail. Supply chain security has moved to the centre: attackers increasingly leapfrog into a target through its suppliers, so a provider must hold its own supply chain to the standards it sets for itself, including knowing which third parties have touched customer data and which the service depends on for hardware and software. Secure service administration matters because administration systems are high-value targets, prized by attackers including cryptocurrency miners. Audit information and alerting for customers is what allows you to establish how and when an incident occurred, rather than merely that one did. And secure use of the service is the customer-side principle: providers should be secure by design and by default, and security should never be an afterthought bolted on at the end.

Why external interfaces matter

A real-world illustration of external interface protection: an outage at a major UK airport, where a failure involving a third-party interface feeding the departures displays caused roughly four hours of disruption. Nothing exotic was attacked; an external dependency failed, and operations felt it immediately. External APIs, web consoles, and command line interfaces are part of the attack and failure surface even when they belong to someone else.

DevOps security

Study guide: Ch 1 › Emerging Technologies › DevOps and DevSecOps

DevOps security, often called DevSecOps, integrates automated security testing directly into the development and deployment pipeline, treating security as a shared responsibility across development and operations teams rather than a separate, later stage handled only by a dedicated security team.

Example

A bank's development pipeline automatically scans every code change for known vulnerabilities before it can be merged, rather than waiting for a manual security review after the application is already built. This catches problems earlier, when they are cheaper and easier to fix.

Two limits are worth keeping in mind. DevSecOps shortens the window between introducing and fixing a defect, driving down mean time to detection, but going faster also risks skipping fundamental security thinking in new features. And DevSecOps does not tell you how to architect security: which identity and access management approach to standardise on, which cryptographic libraries to permit, how key management works, or how sensitive data is encrypted at rest and in motion. Those remain architecture decisions that the pipeline merely enforces.

Common question

How do DevOps and DevSecOps differ? DevOps does not always incorporate security into the framework, while DevSecOps explicitly does: the developer's code is security-checked as it is written, with the whole process automated. The practical warning: the two terms are often used interchangeably, including in exam material, so read questions about DevOps with the possibility in mind that DevSecOps practices are what is being described.

Why this matters for the exam

  • Keep RTO and RPO straight. RTO is about time to recover. RPO is about how much data you can lose. Scenario questions often give you two numbers and ask you to label them correctly.
  • Remember that a BIA identifies critical functions first, then assesses impact, then derives recovery objectives. Reversing this order is a common distractor pattern.
  • Watch for scenarios that describe IaaS versus SaaS responsibility incorrectly, such as suggesting a SaaS customer must patch the operating system. That responsibility sits with the provider in a typical SaaS arrangement.

Verifying a provider's security posture before you commit

Verification against criteria

Study guide: Ch 10 › Cloud Contract Design › Vendor Management

Before trusting a provider with sensitive data or critical workloads, an organization needs a structured way to evaluate that provider's security posture against its own requirements. This is not a one time check. It typically draws on a mix of certifications, audit reports, and direct evidence, compared consistently across candidate providers.

Example

A bank's procurement team builds a scorecard covering encryption practices, certifications held, audit report availability, incident history, and financial stability, then applies that same scorecard to every provider under consideration. This gives a repeatable, defensible basis for the final decision, rather than relying on a sales presentation alone.

Two further perspectives round out provider evaluation: how a mature assurance programme verifies in practice, and what holding certifications signals to attackers.

Example from the field

Rackspace's third-party assurance programme around 2018 offers a practical model: roughly 180 suppliers audited over a twelve-month period. The method matters more than the number. A supplier's ISO 27001 certificate was never taken at face value; the team scratched beneath the surface and asked for visibility of the key documents behind it, the Statement of Applicability, the risk register, and the risk treatment plan, before any personal data was shared. The goal was parity: confirming the supplier handled, stored, and processed personal data to the same standard, with matching SLAs for breach notification. A certificate tells you a management system exists; the documents tell you what it actually covers.

Why certifications deter attackers

Certifications are also signalling. Threat actors run their own return on investment calculations, and cybercrime now operates at the scale of a major economy, by one widely cited estimate the world's third largest, behind only the United States and China. For most attackers the scarce resource is time, so visible assurance such as Cyber Essentials, Cyber Essentials Plus, or ISO 27001 pushes opportunistic attackers down the list toward softer targets. It is not a defence in itself, but it changes the economics of being chosen.

Common certifications and reports

Study guide: Ch 9 › Audit Reports; Ch 10 › Government Cloud Standards (Common Criteria, FIPS 140-2)

Several certifications and report types come up repeatedly, and the exam expects you to know what each one actually covers, since they are often confused with one another.

  • ISO/IEC 27001, an internationally recognized standard for an information security management system, covering areas such as risk assessment, asset management, and access control at the organizational level. In practiceCertification runs on a three-year cycle with annual surveillance audits, and the current Annex A lists 93 controls spanning people, process, and technology.
  • SOC 2 Type I, an assessment of whether a provider's controls are suitably designed at a single point in time.
  • SOC 2 Type II, an assessment of whether those controls operated effectively over a defined period, typically six to twelve months. This gives stronger assurance than Type I, since it demonstrates sustained effectiveness rather than design alone.
  • FIPS 140-2, and its successor FIPS 140-3, a standard for validating the security of cryptographic modules, including hardware security modules. In practiceFIPS 140-2 defines four security levels for cryptographic modules, covering areas such as ports and interfaces, roles and services, physical security, and design assurance.
  • ISO/IEC 27002, the companion to 27001, giving implementation guidance for the controls rather than the management system requirements themselves.
  • ISO/IEC 27017, cloud specific security controls guidance, extending 27002 with additional controls for both providers and customers.
  • SOC 1, which reports on controls relevant to financial reporting, as distinct from SOC 2, which reports on security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy. SOC 3 is a public summary of a SOC 2, suitable for general distribution but too high level for real assurance work.
  • NIST SP 800-53, a comprehensive catalogue of security and privacy controls for federal information systems, widely used well beyond US government.
  • PCI DSS, mandatory for any organization that accepts, transmits, or stores cardholder data, regardless of its size or transaction volume.
  • Common Criteria, an internationally recognized framework for evaluating the security assurance of IT products, often referenced by governments and highly regulated organizations. It has two key components: protection profiles, which state the security requirements for a category of product, and Evaluation Assurance Levels (EALs), which express how rigorously the product was evaluated. A higher EAL means more thorough evaluation, not necessarily a more secure product.
  • CSA STAR, a registry built on the Cloud Controls Matrix. STAR Level 1 relies on the provider's own self-assessment. STAR Level 2 involves an independent third party audit, which offers stronger assurance.
Example

A telecom company's security team specifically requests a SOC 2 Type II report from a prospective provider, rather than accepting a Type I report, because the team wants evidence that the provider's controls have actually worked as intended over the past year, not just that they were well designed on paper.

Watch this in the exam

Common Criteria has two components: protection profiles, which describe the security requirements for a class of product in a given context, and Evaluation Assurance Levels. Evaluation Assurance Levels run from EAL1 to EAL7, with EAL7 the most rigorously evaluated; a common error is remembering the scale as running only one to five. Products are examined against their protection profile, including for embedded malicious functionality, before governments will rely on them.

Vendor viability

Study guide: Ch 10 › Cloud Contract Design › Vendor Management

Technical certifications matter, but they are not the whole picture. A provider's financial stability and long term prospects as a going concern are also part of a thorough evaluation. A technically excellent provider that goes out of business in two years creates its own serious risk.

Example

A maritime company's risk team factors a smaller provider's financial statements and market position into its evaluation, alongside that provider's technical certifications, since a sudden business failure could disrupt operations just as severely as a security incident.

Why this matters for the exam

  • Do not confuse SOC 2 Type I with Type II. Type I is a design check at a point in time. Type II is an effectiveness check over a period. This distinction appears often.
  • FIPS 140-2 certifies cryptographic modules specifically. It is not a general organizational security certification the way ISO/IEC 27001 is.
  • STAR Level 2 involves independent audit. STAR Level 1 is self-assessed. If a question emphasizes independent verification, it is pointing toward Level 2.
  • Keep the SOC family straight. SOC 1 is financial reporting, SOC 2 is the security and privacy trust criteria, and SOC 3 is a public summary of a SOC 2. Only SOC 2, and specifically Type II, gives you evidence of controls operating over time.
  • A higher Common Criteria EAL means the product was evaluated more rigorously, not that it is inherently more secure. This distinction is a favourite of exam writers.
  • A certification is only meaningful within its stated scope. Always check which services, regions, and facilities a certificate actually covers before relying on it.

It covers five areas: cloud threat detection and analysis, data source validation and verification, Security Orchestration, Automation and Response, ethical concerns, and regulatory requirements. AI also appears woven through the other five domains, but this section establishes the foundational concepts.

AI in cloud threat detection and analysis

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition. Ch 1 › Emerging Technologies › Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence gives background only.

Machine learning models can process far more security telemetry than a human team could review manually, which makes them useful for spotting patterns and anomalies across large, multi-cloud environments. The intent is to let AI handle the volume, so human analysts can focus their attention on the complex, ambiguous cases that genuinely need judgement.

Example

A bank's security operations centre uses a machine learning model to automatically flag unusual login patterns across its cloud accounts, such as a login from an unfamiliar country followed immediately by an attempt to download a large volume of customer data. The model surfaces this pattern for a human analyst to investigate, rather than the analyst having to manually review every login across the entire environment.

Example from the field

Machine learning driven anti-malware illustrates the scale advantage: modern engines examine three hundred to five hundred features and characteristics of a file before it even executes, seeded with known patterns and then adapting continuously, which no signature list maintained by humans could match. Anthropic's Project Glasswing, a programme applying AI agents to vulnerability discovery in critical national infrastructure systems, including finding new vulnerabilities in systems that have been under human assessment for decades, shows where AI-assisted security analysis is heading.

Data source validation and verification

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

An AI model is only as trustworthy as the data it learns from and operates on. Data poisoning prevention means validating, sanitizing, and verifying the integrity of training data, so a model does not learn from corrupted or maliciously altered inputs. This concern extends to the data a deployed model consumes in production, not just the data used during initial training.

Example

An energy company's security team regularly checks the threat intelligence feeds used to train its anomaly detection model, confirming the feeds come from reputable sources and have not been tampered with. If those feeds were quietly poisoned with misleading data, the model's future detections could become unreliable in ways that are hard to notice until real damage occurs.

Security Orchestration, Automation and Response

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

SOAR platforms use automation, often enhanced by AI, to correlate security events and trigger predefined response actions with minimal manual effort. This does not replace incident response procedures. It accelerates the routine parts of them, so analysts spend their time on the incidents that genuinely require human judgement.

Example

When a SOAR platform detects a pattern strongly associated with a known type of attack, such as repeated failed logins followed by a successful one from an unusual location, it can automatically disable the affected account and alert an analyst, rather than waiting for a person to notice the pattern manually and take action.

Ethical concerns

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

When an AI system makes or influences decisions that affect people, questions of fairness become a security and governance concern, not just a technical one. Bias tracking involves monitoring a model's outputs across different groups to identify discriminatory patterns. Algorithmic transparency, often called explainability, is the ability to explain how a model reached a particular decision, which matters both for regulatory compliance and for maintaining trust with the people affected by that decision.

Example

A telecom company uses an AI model to help decide which customers receive automated offers of a payment plan when they fall behind on their bill. The company monitors whether the model treats customers from different backgrounds consistently, and it keeps a record of the key factors behind each decision, so it can explain a specific outcome if a customer or a regulator asks.

Regulatory requirements

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

Different jurisdictions increasingly impose their own rules on automated decision making, including explainability obligations under frameworks such as GDPR and emerging AI specific legislation. An organization operating across several countries needs to understand and satisfy each applicable regime, rather than assuming one set of rules covers every market.

Example

A bank operating in both the UK and the EU has to account for how each jurisdiction's rules on automated decision making apply to its AI powered credit scoring system, since the specific obligations, such as a customer's right to an explanation, may differ between the two.

Shared responsibility for AI as a Service

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition. The general shared responsibility model is Ch 5 › Shared Responsibilities by Service Type.

When an organization uses a cloud provider's AI as a Service offering, the shared responsibility model still applies, but the split can differ from a provider's standard IaaS or PaaS terms. Generally, the provider is responsible for the underlying model infrastructure and platform security, while the customer remains responsible for the data it feeds into the model, the model's configuration, and the appropriate governance of the outputs it produces. This boundary should always be reviewed specifically for the AI offering in question, rather than assumed to match the provider's other services.

Example

A bank hosts a fraud detection model using a provider's AI as a Service offering. The provider secures the servers and platform the model runs on, but the bank remains responsible for the quality of the transaction data it feeds into the model and for making sure the model's fraud alerts are used appropriately by its own staff.

Securing the AI training environment

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

Training a large model can require significant compute resources running for extended periods, which makes the training environment itself an attractive target. Logical isolation, often through micro-segmentation, limits the damage if another workload sharing the same cloud account is compromised. Infrastructure as Code allows the environment's configuration to be version controlled and consistently applied before resources are provisioned, reducing the risk of manual misconfiguration.

Example

A bank isolates the network segment used for training its fraud detection model from the rest of its cloud environment, so that even if an unrelated application elsewhere in the same account is compromised, the attacker cannot easily reach the training cluster or the sensitive data it processes.

Model inversion and extraction

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

A deployed model can itself become a target. Model inversion and extraction attacks involve an adversary carefully analyzing a model's outputs over many queries, attempting to reconstruct details of the training data or to replicate the model's functionality without authorization. Rate limiting and monitoring the inference API for unusual query patterns are practical defences against this kind of attack.

Example

An attacker sends thousands of carefully varied queries to a bank's public facing credit scoring API, hoping to reverse engineer how the underlying model makes its decisions. Rate limiting the number of queries a single account can make, combined with monitoring for this kind of systematic querying pattern, helps the bank detect and stop the attempt before enough information is extracted to be useful.

Model drift

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

A model's performance can gradually change over time as real world data patterns shift away from the conditions it was originally trained on. This is called model drift, and it is a security concern as well as a data science one, since a sudden or unexplained change in a model's outputs could indicate anything from a benign shift in customer behaviour to a deliberate manipulation of the data the model relies on. Continuous monitoring of model performance is the standard way to catch drift early.

Example

A bank's fraud detection model starts generating noticeably more false positives over several weeks, with no obvious change in actual fraud patterns. Rather than dismissing this as routine noise, the security team investigates it as a potential incident, since it could point to a subtle problem with the data the model is now receiving.

Core AI and ML vocabulary

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition. Ch 1 › Emerging Technologies › Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence gives background only.

The outline assumes familiarity with the terms, and questions often turn on distinguishing them. A model is the artefact produced by training, holding learned parameters or weights. Training is the process of deriving those weights from a dataset; inference is using the finished model to produce an output for a new input. Fine-tuning adapts an existing pre-trained model with additional, usually narrower, data rather than training from scratch. A foundation model is a large general-purpose model intended to be adapted for many downstream uses.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) supplies a model with relevant documents retrieved at query time rather than relying only on what it learned during training, which keeps answers current without retraining but introduces the retrieval store as a new attack surface. An agent is a model given tools and the autonomy to call them, which converts a system that produces text into one that takes actions.

Example

A bank uses a foundation model, fine-tunes it on its own policy documents, and deploys it with RAG over a live product database. Three distinct trust boundaries now exist: the vendor's original training data, the bank's fine-tuning corpus, and the documents retrieved at query time. A weakness in any of them reaches the answer the customer sees.

AI governance frameworks

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

Because the 3rd Edition study guide predates this material entirely, these frameworks are worth knowing by name and purpose.

  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF), a voluntary framework structured around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. Govern establishes the culture and accountability, Map establishes context and identifies risks, Measure analyses and tracks them, and Manage allocates resources to treat them. In practiceAn energy company uses it to demonstrate to its board that AI risk is handled through the same discipline as other enterprise risk rather than as a separate experiment.
  • ISO/IEC 42001, the international standard for an AI management system, structured like ISO/IEC 27001 but for AI. It is certifiable, which matters when customers or regulators want independent evidence rather than self-assertion. In practiceA cloud provider certifies against it so customers can rely on an attestation rather than auditing the provider's AI practices themselves.
  • EU AI Act, risk-tiered legislation classifying systems as unacceptable, high, limited, or minimal risk, with obligations escalating accordingly. High-risk systems carry requirements covering data governance, technical documentation, human oversight, accuracy, and robustness. In practiceAn AI system screening job applicants falls into the high-risk tier, so the employer must document its data, provide meaningful human oversight, and be able to explain outcomes.
  • MITRE ATLAS, an adversarial threat knowledge base for AI systems, structured like MITRE ATT&CK but cataloguing tactics and techniques used against machine learning. In practiceA security team uses it to build test cases for AI-specific attacks the way it already uses ATT&CK for conventional intrusion techniques.
Watch this in the exam

Keep the four apart by what they are. NIST AI RMF is a voluntary risk process. ISO/IEC 42001 is a certifiable management system. The EU AI Act is binding law with risk tiers. MITRE ATLAS is a threat catalogue, not a governance framework at all.

Adversarial attacks against AI systems

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

Beyond model inversion and extraction covered above, several attack classes recur and are distinguished by which phase they target and what the attacker gains.

  • Data poisoning, corrupting training data so the finished model behaves as the attacker intends. Targets the training phase. ExampleContributing crafted samples to a public dataset so a fraud model learns to treat one transaction pattern as legitimate.
  • Adversarial examples (evasion), crafting an input with small perturbations that cause misclassification at inference time, often imperceptible to a human. ExampleModifying a handful of pixels so a malware image classifier reports a malicious file as benign.
  • Membership inference, determining whether a specific individual's record was part of the training set, which is a privacy breach even when the record itself is not recovered. ExampleEstablishing that a named patient's record was in a hospital's training data, thereby revealing they were treated there.
  • Model extraction (stealing), querying a deployed model systematically to reconstruct an equivalent model, taking the intellectual property without the training cost. ExampleDriving a public pricing API with thousands of varied queries until the pricing logic can be replicated.
  • Model inversion, reconstructing attributes of training data from model outputs. ExampleRecovering recognisable features of faces used to train a facial recognition model.

AI supply chain and transparency artefacts

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition. Conventional supply chain is Ch 6 › Supply Chain Management and Licensing.

An AI system inherits risk from every model, dataset, and library it depends on, most of which the organisation did not create. Two artefacts address this.

  • AI Bill of Materials (AI-BOM), extending the SBOM concept to record the models, datasets, and frameworks a system depends on, so that when a public model or dataset is found to be compromised the affected systems can be identified quickly.
  • Model cards and datasheets, structured documentation describing a model's intended use, training data, evaluation results, limitations, and known biases, which supports both governance and the explainability obligations regulators increasingly impose.
Example

A widely used open model checkpoint is found to carry an implanted backdoor. An organisation with an AI-BOM identifies within minutes which of its services fine-tuned from it. An organisation without one begins a manual investigation across every team that has touched machine learning.

Guardrails and AI red teaming

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

Guardrails are controls placed around a model rather than inside it, filtering what reaches it and what it returns. Input guardrails screen for prompt injection and disallowed requests; output guardrails screen for sensitive data disclosure, unsafe content, and malformed responses before anything downstream consumes them. Because the model itself is probabilistic and cannot be guaranteed to behave, guardrails are where deterministic enforcement lives.

AI red teaming is structured adversarial testing of an AI system, probing for prompt injection, data leakage, unsafe outputs, and excessive agency. It differs from conventional penetration testing in that the same input may produce different outputs on different runs, so findings are expressed as likelihood across repeated attempts rather than as a single reproducible exploit.

Example

A bank's red team finds that one phrasing extracts internal policy text from its assistant roughly one attempt in twenty. A conventional penetration test might have run it once, seen a refusal, and recorded a pass. The probabilistic nature of the system is precisely why repeated testing matters.

Securing agentic AI

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

An agent that can call tools can take actions with real consequences, which changes the risk profile substantially. Three controls matter most: least privilege for tools, granting the agent only the specific capabilities its task requires rather than broad access; identity per agent, so actions are attributable and revocable individually rather than hidden behind a shared service account; and human-in-the-loop approval for consequential or irreversible actions.

Example

A maritime company deploys an agent to reconcile shipping invoices. Given write access to the finance system it could, if manipulated through a crafted invoice, alter payment details. Restricted to read access with a human approving any change, the same manipulation produces a flagged anomaly rather than a fraudulent payment.

Why this matters for the exam

  • Do not assume AI replaces human judgement anywhere in the CCSP body of knowledge. Every reference to AI in the outline positions it as a tool that assists and scales human effort, not a replacement for it.
  • Keep data poisoning prevention, which concerns training data integrity, separate from model inversion and extraction, which concerns attacks against a model's outputs. They sit at different points in the AI lifecycle.
  • Remember that AIaaS shared responsibility should be reviewed on its own terms for each specific offering, rather than assumed to be identical to a provider's standard IaaS or PaaS split.
