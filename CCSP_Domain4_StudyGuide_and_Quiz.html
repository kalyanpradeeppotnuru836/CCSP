4.1 Training and awareness for application security Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 6, Cloud Application Security (training and awareness, common pitfalls, and common vulnerabilities sections). The OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications is new to the August 2026 outline and is not covered in the 3rd Edition.

Building a security-aware development culture

Cloud development basics

Study guide: Ch 6 › Developing Software for the Cloud

The Cloud Security Alliance and SAFECode identify collaborative accountability as the foundation of a security-conscious development programme, built on three components:

  • Security by design, treating security as a fundamental consideration at every stage, from initial feasibility through to retirement, rather than an afterthought or a reaction to a discovered flaw.
  • Shared security responsibility, where everyone from senior management to the newest team member holds accountability, rather than security being one team's problem.
  • Security as a business objective, where security is central to business operations rather than a part of them, and every business goal carries a security element.

Common pitfalls

Study guide: Ch 6 › Common Cloud Application Deployment Pitfalls

The primary pitfall is failure to secure immediate and ongoing support from upper management. If senior leadership does not approve and adhere to security policies, and does not adequately fund and staff security work aligned to organisational goals, efforts to build a security culture will fail. Other recurring pitfalls include not understanding the organisation's culture, since even businesses in the same sector differ; inconsistency across teams when no established secure development framework exists; and cutting training programmes when there is no immediate consequence. A committed organisation finds ways to sustain awareness training under financial pressure, for example through online course subscriptions rather than abandoning it.

Example

A bank mandates secure coding standards but does not fund the training or give teams time to apply them. Developers meet delivery deadlines by treating the standard as optional. The policy exists on paper and fails in practice, which is exactly what inadequate leadership commitment produces.

Two arguments help when training budgets come under pressure. Frame the case in terms the board already works in: the upper tier of GDPR fines, and the reputational damage that follows a breach handled badly. And recognise that awareness training is one of the highest-return interventions available for raising an organisation's security maturity, precisely because reconnaissance and social engineering now account for so much of the early stages of the kill chain. The perimeter that matters most is the eyes and ears of employees, and it is the only one that cannot be bought.

Where security expertise is scarce, distributing it works better than concentrating it. Rather than holding all specialists in one team, embed them into development teams for the secure SDLC, or make them available as internal consultants. Identifying and upskilling people across departments who already have an interest in security, sometimes called security champions, extends reach without extending headcount. For anyone wanting to understand the social engineering side properly, the writings of Kevin Mitnick, including Ghost in the Wires and The Art of Deception, are the standard first-hand accounts.

Common cloud vulnerabilities and the reference lists

Study guide: Ch 6 › Cloud Application Assurance and Validation › Common Threats to Applications

Cloud's reliance on network systems exposes it to data breaches, compromised integrity, insecure APIs, and denial of service, with applications and transmissions operating in potentially hostile environments. Five reference lists are named in the outline. Each is set out in full below, since the exam expects recognition of individual entries rather than the list's general purpose.

OWASP Top 10 (web applications)

Study guide: Ch 6 › Common Threats to Applications

The most widely used catalogue of critical web application security risks. The current edition is organised as ten categories rather than ten individual bugs.

  • A01 Broken Access Control, users acting outside their intended permissions. Examplechanging an account number in a URL and viewing another customer's statement.
  • A02 Cryptographic Failures, sensitive data exposed through weak, missing, or misapplied cryptography. Examplecard numbers transmitted over unencrypted HTTP, or stored using an obsolete hashing algorithm.
  • A03 Injection, untrusted input interpreted as a command, covering SQL, command, and cross-site scripting injection. Examplea login form where entering a crafted string returns every row in the users table.
  • A04 Insecure Design, flaws originating in the design itself rather than the implementation, which no amount of correct coding can fix. Examplea password reset flow relying solely on a security question that is publicly discoverable.
  • A05 Security Misconfiguration, insecure defaults, incomplete hardening, verbose errors, or unnecessary features left enabled. Examplea cloud storage bucket left publicly readable, or an admin console reachable with factory credentials.
  • A06 Vulnerable and Outdated Components, using libraries, frameworks, or runtimes with known vulnerabilities. Examplean application still shipping a logging library with a published remote code execution flaw.
  • A07 Identification and Authentication Failures, weaknesses in confirming who a user is, including credential stuffing and poor session management. Examplesession identifiers that never expire and are accepted indefinitely after logout.
  • A08 Software and Data Integrity Failures, trusting code or data whose integrity has not been verified, including insecure deserialisation and unsigned updates. Examplea build pipeline pulling an unsigned dependency from an unverified source.
  • A09 Security Logging and Monitoring Failures, insufficient recording or alerting, so breaches go undetected. Exampleauthentication failures never logged, leaving a months-long brute force attempt invisible.
  • A10 Server-Side Request Forgery (SSRF), an application fetching a remote resource from a user-supplied URL without validation. Examplesubmitting an internal metadata service address so the server retrieves cloud credentials and returns them.

Mitigations for the cloud-relevant entries

Knowing the risk is half the requirement; the exam also expects the control that addresses it.

  • A01 Broken Access Control, enforce least privilege in IAM policies by default, run regular access reviews, and use policy as code so permissions cannot drift silently.
  • A02 Cryptographic Failures, encrypt at rest and in transit by default, use the provider's key management service with proper rotation, and retire deprecated protocols; TLS 1.2 or above only.
  • A03 Injection, parameterised queries and prepared statements, input validation and sanitisation, WAF rules, and avoiding dynamic query construction in serverless functions.
  • A04 Insecure Design, threat modelling during design, architecture review, and security requirements agreed before coding starts. This one cannot be patched later, which is the whole point of the category.
  • A05 Security Misconfiguration, infrastructure as code with security baselines, cloud security posture management tools scanning continuously for drift, disabling default accounts and unused services, and automated hardening templates.
  • A06 Vulnerable and Outdated Components, software composition analysis, a maintained patch schedule, and minimal hardened base container images.
  • A07 Identification and Authentication Failures, enforce MFA, strong session management, rate limiting on login attempts, and federated identity rather than local credential stores.
  • A08 Software and Data Integrity Failures, code signing, verifying package checksums and signatures, restricting write access to CI/CD pipelines, and reviewing the software bill of materials.
  • A09 Security Logging and Monitoring Failures, centralise cloud-native logs, integrate them with a SIEM for real-time alerting, and define and test incident response playbooks.
  • A10 Server-Side Request Forgery, restrict outbound requests through allow lists, protect cloud metadata endpoints, and segment the application tier from internal services.

Two observations follow from that list. Several of these entries sit squarely on the customer side of the shared responsibility model in IaaS and PaaS, security misconfiguration, broken access control, and cryptographic failures above all, and the exam likes to test that boundary. And SSRF is the most cloud-specific entry: added in the 2021 edition, it targets internal metadata services to steal credentials and has no real equivalent in a traditional on-premises estate. The list is not ranked by real-world frequency in cloud either; if it were, security misconfiguration would be first.

Watch this in the exam

A01 Broken Access Control and A07 Identification and Authentication Failures are frequently confused. Authentication asks who you are; access control asks what you may do once identified. A user correctly logged in who then reaches another customer's data is an access control failure, not an authentication one.

OWASP Application Security Verification Standard (ASVS)

Study guide: Ch 6 › Quality Assurance and Testing Techniques

Not a risk list but a verification framework, giving developers and testers a common standard of security requirements to assess an application against. It is organised into levels of increasing rigour, so an organisation can state which level a given application must meet. Where the Top 10 raises awareness of what commonly goes wrong, ASVS defines what must be demonstrably right.

OWASP Top 10 API Security Risks

Study guide: Ch 6 › Application Programming Interfaces; Common Threats to Applications

APIs fail differently from web front ends, chiefly because authorisation must be enforced per object and per function on every endpoint rather than once at a page boundary.

  • API1 Broken Object Level Authorization, an endpoint returning any object whose identifier is supplied, without checking ownership. Examplerequesting /orders/1002 and receiving another customer's order.
  • API2 Broken Authentication, weak or missing verification of the caller's identity. Examplean API accepting an expired token because expiry is never validated.
  • API3 Broken Object Property Level Authorization, exposing or accepting fields the caller should not see or set, covering excessive data exposure and mass assignment. Examplea profile endpoint returning the full record including an internal creditRating field, or accepting isAdmin=true in an update.
  • API4 Unrestricted Resource Consumption, no limits on requests, payload size, or downstream cost. Examplean unthrottled search endpoint driven at volume until the database and the bill both collapse.
  • API5 Broken Function Level Authorization, administrative functions reachable by ordinary users because authorisation is enforced in the interface rather than the API. Examplecalling DELETE /users/44 directly as a standard user.
  • API6 Unrestricted Access to Sensitive Business Flows, automation abusing a legitimate flow at scale. Examplebots completing a ticket purchase flow thousands of times to corner inventory.
  • API7 Server Side Request Forgery, as in the web list, but especially damaging in APIs that routinely fetch remote resources on request.
  • API8 Security Misconfiguration, permissive CORS policies, missing security headers, unnecessary HTTP methods, or verbose errors. Examplea CORS policy allowing any origin to call an authenticated endpoint.
  • API9 Improper Inventory Management, forgotten, undocumented, or deprecated endpoints left running. Examplean old v1 API still live without the authorisation fixes applied to v2.
  • API10 Unsafe Consumption of APIs, trusting responses from third-party APIs without validation. Examplepassing a partner API's response straight into a database query.

OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications

Study guide: Not covered in the 3rd Edition.

New to this exam outline. The current edition is the 2025 release, and several categories were renamed from the original 2023 list, so material written before then uses different names for the same risks.

  • LLM01 Prompt Injection, crafted input altering the model's behaviour or causing it to disregard its instructions, whether supplied directly by a user or indirectly through content the model reads. Examplea web page containing hidden text instructing a summarising agent to exfiltrate the user's data.
  • LLM02 Sensitive Information Disclosure, secrets, personal data, or confidential material leaking through model outputs. Examplea support assistant reproducing another customer's details that appeared in its context.
  • LLM03 Supply Chain, compromised or vulnerable third-party models, datasets, libraries, or hosting. Examplefine-tuning on a public model checkpoint that carries an implanted backdoor.
  • LLM04 Data and Model Poisoning, deliberate manipulation of training, fine-tuning, or retrieval data to shape behaviour. Exampleseeding a public dataset so the resulting model misclassifies a specific fraud pattern as legitimate.
  • LLM05 Improper Output Handling, downstream systems trusting model output without validation. Examplerendering a model's response into a dashboard unsanitised, so embedded script executes in an analyst's browser.
  • LLM06 Excessive Agency, granting the model or agent more capability, autonomy, or permission than is safe. Examplean agent given write access to a production database when read access would serve.
  • LLM07 System Prompt Leakage, internal prompts, policies, or tool definitions being extracted. Examplea user coaxing the assistant into reprinting its configuration, revealing which controls it enforces and how to evade them.
  • LLM08 Vector and Embedding Weaknesses, attack and leakage paths introduced by retrieval-augmented generation, indexing, and embeddings. Exampleweak tenant separation in a vector database returning another customer's documents in a retrieval result.
  • LLM09 Misinformation, confidently incorrect output being acted upon as fact. Examplean assistant inventing a regulatory citation that staff then rely on in a compliance filing.
  • LLM10 Unbounded Consumption, cost, latency, or capacity exhausted through abuse or missing limits. Examplean unauthenticated endpoint driven with long prompts until inference spend runs away.
Watch this in the exam

Keep prompt injection and improper output handling apart, since both appear together in options. Prompt injection is manipulation arriving on the way in. Improper output handling is failure to validate on the way out. A single incident often involves both, and the question will describe which end it means. Note also that older material calls LLM05 insecure output handling, LLM04 training data poisoning, and LLM10 model denial of service.

SANS Top 25 (CWE Most Dangerous Software Errors)

Study guide: Ch 6 › Common Threats to Applications

Maintained as the CWE Top 25, this list catalogues the specific coding weaknesses behind most exploited vulnerabilities. Where the OWASP lists describe risks in deployed applications, this one names the underlying implementation defects, which is why it is aimed at developers rather than architects. Recurring entries include out-of-bounds write and out-of-bounds read, cross-site scripting, SQL injection, use after free, OS command injection, improper input validation, cross-site request forgery, path traversal, unrestricted upload of dangerous file types, missing authorisation, integer overflow, deserialisation of untrusted data, and use of hard-coded credentials. For the exam, recognise its purpose and its developer orientation rather than memorising the ranking, which is re-ordered annually.

Watch this in the exam

Do not confuse prompt injection with improper output handling. Prompt injection is crafted input manipulating the model into ignoring its instructions. Improper output handling is the application failing to sanitise what the model produced before using it downstream. They sit at opposite ends of the same request, and questions often describe one while offering both.

How this gets examined

Questions on these lists do not follow one pattern. Some ask for the root cause of a described failure, some ask which category a scenario belongs to, and some are scenario-based and test whether you are reading carefully rather than recognising a keyword. Two habits help: read each question more than once before looking at the options, since the answer is often signalled in the wording of the question itself, and expect more than one option to look defensible. The task is to select the most appropriate answer, not the only possible one.

4.2 The Secure Software Development Life Cycle Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 6, Cloud Application Security (SDLC business requirements, phases, and methodologies sections).

Requirements, phases, and methodologies

Business requirements

Study guide: Ch 6 › Cloud-Secure Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC)

Gathering, reviewing, and understanding business requirements is essential to meeting organisational goals and customer expectations, whether for a new system or an improvement to an existing one. At conception, the requirements must rest on a comprehensive understanding of the project's necessity, its objectives, and its target audience, and should incorporate feedback from as many stakeholders as possible. Security requirements belong in this gathering alongside functional ones, because a requirement discovered late costs far more to satisfy than one captured at the start.

Phases

Study guide: Ch 6 › Software Development Phases

Any SDLC, including a secure SDLC, runs through a common set of phases: requirements gathering, design, development or coding, testing, deployment, and maintenance through to eventual retirement. What makes an SSDLC distinct is that security activity attaches to every phase rather than appearing only at testing.

Two phases repay closer attention. At deployment, the work is the initial release, and the security task is ensuring default configurations conform to security requirements and good practice, including the configuration of APIs and of identity and access management. Getting those defaults right is what reduces the risk of credential compromise and protects account creation, maintenance, and installation processes. Where an application consumes several cloud services, produce a standard configuration guide for each service approved for use, so that every team configures it the same way. At operations and maintenance, remember that this is the longest phase of the entire life cycle, covering everything after release including operational monitoring and ongoing maintenance; most of an application's security life happens here rather than during development.

Methodologies

Study guide: Ch 6 › Software Development Models
  • Waterfall, a sequential and linear approach where each phase completes before the next begins. Predictable and well documented, but slow to accommodate change once underway.
  • Agile, iterative and incremental, delivering working software in short cycles and adapting to changing requirements. Security must be embedded in each iteration rather than deferred.
  • Spiral, iterative with explicit risk analysis at each cycle, making it well suited to projects where risk must be actively managed.
  • DevOps, the more recent approach enhancing deployment frequency and speed by integrating development and operations, with DevSecOps adding security into that integration.
Example

A telecom company building a regulated billing platform uses waterfall, because the requirements are fixed by regulation and a documented sequential trail supports the audit. Its customer-facing app team uses agile, because requirements shift with market feedback. Both need security in every phase; only the cadence differs.

Why staging matters

In 2024 a widely deployed endpoint security product distributed a faulty content update to production systems worldwide without adequate validation in a non-production environment. The result was large-scale disruption reaching critical national infrastructure, including aviation and healthcare, from a change that was not itself malicious. The reputational damage was contained because the vendor identified and rolled back the change quickly, which is the second lesson: the ability to reverse a deployment is as much a security control as the testing that should have caught it.

4.3 Applying the Secure SDLC Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 6, Cloud Application Security (cloud-specific risks, threat modeling, secure coding, and configuration management sections).

Cloud-specific risks, threat modeling, secure coding, and configuration management

Cloud-specific risks

Study guide: Ch 6 › Common Threats to Applications; Ch 4 › Cloud Computing Risks by Service Model

Risks that arise specifically from using cloud services include data breaches; insufficient identity, credential, and access management; insecure interfaces and APIs; system vulnerabilities; compliance risks; data sovereignty; vendor lock-in; service reliability; scalability issues; cost management; and integration challenges. The outline also names shared technology issues, provider insider threats, lack of visibility and control, and legal and jurisdictional issues.

Threat modeling techniques

Study guide: Ch 6 › Cloud Application Assurance and Validation › Threat Modeling

Four techniques are named, and the exam relies on you distinguishing what each actually does:

  • STRIDE, a categorisation scheme identifying threats as Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, or Elevation of Privilege. It tells you what kinds of threat exist.
  • DREAD, a rating scheme scoring identified threats on Damage potential, Reproducibility, Exploitability, Affected users, and Discoverability. It tells you how serious each threat is, so it follows identification rather than replacing it.
  • ATASM, Architecture, Threats, Attack Surfaces, and Mitigations, working from the architecture outward to identify where the system can be attacked and what should defend it.
  • PASTA, the Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis, a seven-stage risk-centric methodology aligning technical threats to business objectives through attacker-focused simulation.

Two of these deserve more detail. PASTA is the one most often encountered in regulated sectors, particularly financial services, because it integrates threat analysis into an organisation-wide risk model rather than treating it as a development activity. It begins by defining business objectives and scope, then works through threat analysis grounded in actual threat intelligence: what attackers are doing in the wild, in your sector, against your technology stack, rather than a generic checklist. That depends on the quality of the intelligence, and not all threat intelligence feeds are created equal. Commercial feeds from established vendors, open-source feeds, and the national feeds published by bodies such as the NCSC differ in coverage and timeliness, and with proprietary feeds you broadly get what you pay for. ATASM contributes attack trees: modelling how an adversary could chain individual vulnerabilities together to reach an objective, which surfaces paths that no single vulnerability rating would flag.

Watch this in the exam

STRIDE categorises, DREAD scores. If a scenario is identifying what could go wrong, it wants STRIDE. If it is prioritising threats already found, it wants DREAD. PASTA is the seven-stage business-aligned one; ATASM starts from architecture and attack surfaces.

Insider threat and the leaver process

Insider risk deserves separating out from the general risk list, because the controls are organisational rather than technical. A malicious insider with administrative rights can cause damage that no perimeter control addresses. In one 2025 case at a financial services firm, an employee installed a logic bomb on the corporate network configured to trigger on a change in system state rather than at a set time; when his administrative account was deleted after his departure, the condition was met and critical data was destroyed, costing the organisation a substantial sum within a single day. The lesson is not about the malware. It is that the communication line between HR and IT administration is a security control: privilege revocation for leavers and movers has to be timely, and for privileged accounts it has to be coordinated with whatever else that account is wired into. Segregation of duties and awareness training are the other mitigations, and insider incidents typically run a longer mean time to detection than external ones because the activity looks authorised.

Example from the field

A UK bank believed it was tracking around eight hundred fraudulent copies of its banking pages. After engaging specialist third-party services and analysis of deep and dark web sources, the true figure proved to be several times higher. The point for account hijacking defence is that an organisation's own view of its attack surface is usually the optimistic one, and external intelligence is what corrects it.

Secure coding standards

Study guide: Ch 6 › Quality Assurance and Testing Techniques

OWASP ASVS provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating the technical security measures in web applications, giving development and testing teams a common, standardised basis for verification. SAFECode is a forum where company executives and technical experts share knowledge and collaborate on developing, improving, and promoting scalable and effective software security programmes, helping businesses establish or improve their own.

Software configuration management and versioning

Study guide: Ch 8 › Change and Configuration Management

Versioning and configuration management matter for any system, and take on added significance in cloud because rapid provisioning and self-elasticity make it easy for environments to drift apart. System stability and cohesiveness become paramount. In traditional data centres, upgrades and patches are applied directly to existing servers and hosts. In cloud, the immutable pattern is preferred: deploy a new, correctly configured instance and retire the old one, so that every running instance matches a known version rather than accumulating undocumented change.

Example

An energy company's auto-scaling group launches new instances from an image. If patches are applied by hand to running machines, every scale-up event reintroduces the unpatched image, and the fleet silently diverges. Rebuilding the image and replacing instances keeps every machine identical and traceable to a version.

4.4 Cloud software assurance and validation Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 6, Cloud Application Security (testing, quality assurance, and abuse case sections). SAST, DAST, IAST, and SCA are named in the current exam outline and are covered here alongside the white, grey, and black-box perspectives.

Testing what was built, and testing how it can be abused

Functional and non-functional testing

Study guide: Ch 6 › Quality Assurance and Testing Techniques

Functional testing is a focused assessment of a particular function or component, checking that the system does what its functional requirements say it should. In cloud projects this carries extra considerations, notably understanding the legal and regulatory requirements that apply. Non-functional testing covers the aspects functional testing does not: performance, usability, and dependability. Where functional testing examines precise behaviours against requirements, non-functional testing establishes how the system performs and whether it is ready for use.

CI/CD processes automate build, test, and deployment so that changes are validated continuously rather than in a large batch before release, which is what makes frequent secure delivery practical.

Knowledge-based testing perspectives

Study guide: Ch 6 › Quality Assurance and Testing Techniques
  • White-box testing, conducted with full knowledge of the internal structure, source code, and design.
  • Grey-box testing, conducted with partial knowledge, typically some design or architecture information but not full source access.
  • Black-box testing, conducted with no internal knowledge, approaching the system as an external attacker would.

The same three perspectives in penetration testing

These perspectives carry over into penetration testing, where the practical differences are cost, duration, and which attacker is being simulated. A white-box test supplies the testers with the organisation's topology, architecture, and in some engagements legitimate administrative credentials, because it simulates an attack from inside: little or no reconnaissance is needed, so the engagement is shorter and cheaper and the effort goes into depth rather than discovery. A black-box test provides nothing, simulating an external attacker who must begin at the reconnaissance stage of the kill chain, which is why it takes longer, costs more, and often extends to social engineering and physical attempts on the organisation's premises, testing the awareness programme as much as the technology. A grey-box test sits between the two, with partial knowledge of defences or architecture but no source code access, and is the usual choice for integration and application penetration testing.

Security testing methodologies

Study guide: Ch 6 › Quality Assurance and Testing Techniques

These are the tool-based methodologies named in the outline, and they are distinguished by what they examine and when:

  • SAST, Static Application Security Testing, analyses source code or binaries without executing them. It finds coding flaws early but cannot see runtime or configuration issues, and produces false positives for code paths that are never reachable.
  • DAST, Dynamic Application Security Testing, tests the running application from the outside without source access, finding runtime and configuration issues that static analysis misses, but only in the paths it manages to exercise.
  • IAST, Interactive Application Security Testing, instruments the running application so it observes behaviour during execution with visibility into the code, combining elements of both static and dynamic approaches.
  • SCA, Software Composition Analysis, identifies third-party and open-source components and flags those with known vulnerabilities or problematic licences. It examines what you imported rather than what you wrote.
Watch this in the exam

Anchor each to what it inspects. SAST reads code without running it. DAST attacks the running app without reading code. IAST instruments the running app and sees both. SCA looks at dependencies, not your own code. A question about a vulnerable third-party library wants SCA, not SAST.

Quality assurance and abuse case testing

Study guide: Ch 6 › Quality Assurance and Testing Techniques

QA encompasses planning, evaluation, and inspection to guarantee the constructed system meets its quality requirements, applying across all cloud deployment models. Abuse case testing inverts the perspective: rather than confirming the software does what it should, testers compile a list of known misuse cases and actively attempt to exploit them using manual or automated attack techniques. It asks what an attacker would try, not what a user is meant to do.

Example

A use case says a customer can transfer funds between their own accounts. The matching abuse case asks what happens if the account identifier is changed to someone else's, if the amount is negative, or if the request is replayed. Functional testing passes the use case; abuse case testing is what finds the broken authorisation check.

4.5 Using verified secure software Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 6, Cloud Application Security (API security, supply chain, and third-party software sections), with vendor management material in Chapter 10.

APIs, supply chain, third-party software, and open source

Securing APIs

Study guide: Ch 6 › Cloud Application Architecture › Application Programming Interfaces

API security is central to overall application security. Introducing inadequately secured APIs, particularly when integrating APIs outside the application's own control or security boundary, poses significant risk and can jeopardise the entire dataset within the application, leading to exposure or loss. Practical controls include strong authentication and authorisation on every endpoint, input validation, rate limiting and throttling, TLS for all traffic, and careful review of what each endpoint returns, since excessive data exposure is a leading API weakness.

Example from the field

In 2021 an attacker used a data scraping service against a major professional network's APIs to collect profile data covering hundreds of millions of users, a very large proportion of its user base, including email addresses, phone numbers, geolocation records, and linked social accounts. The root cause was not a broken authentication control or an unpatched server: the API permitted mass, unthrottled scraping of profile data with no meaningful rate limiting and no anomaly detection. It is the textbook case of API4 Unrestricted Resource Consumption, and it makes a point worth carrying into the exam: data that is individually public can still constitute a serious breach when an API allows it to be harvested at scale. Treating root cause rather than symptom is what stops this recurring, otherwise incident response becomes a game of whack-a-mole against the same systemic flaw.

Supply chain management

Study guide: Ch 6 › Supply Chain Management and Licensing

Supply chain management has two aspects. First, a provider, and potentially the customer, will engage third-party providers to deliver services, so a provider often relies on other vendors to supply what its customers ultimately consume. This encompasses vendor risk management when establishing those relationships. Second, cloud itself extends the reach of supply chain management, since globally interconnected supply chains need access to primary and secondary sources and cloud infrastructure improves the efficiency of coordinating them. The security concern is that your risk extends through providers you have no contract with.

Example

A bank contracts with a SaaS vendor that depends on a third-party analytics service, which in turn runs on another provider's infrastructure. The bank has a contract with one party but exposure through three. Vendor assessment must ask what the vendor's own dependencies are, not just what the vendor does.

Examples from the field

Two cases make the supply chain argument better than any policy statement. In the 2013 breach of a large US retailer, attackers entered through a compromised heating, ventilation, and air conditioning contractor holding excessive network access, then pivoted from there into payment systems, resulting in the theft of tens of millions of card records. In a later incident at a casino, the entry point was an internet-connected sensor managing a reception aquarium, installed by a third party and never segmented from the production network. Neither supplier was an IT security vendor, and neither would have appeared on a list of critical technology dependencies. Third-party risk assessment has to cover any supplier with network access, not only the ones selling software, and network segmentation is what limits the damage when the assessment misses one.

Third-party software management and open source

Study guide: Ch 5 › Securing Software › Third-Party Software Management; Validating Open-Source Software

Cloud shifts software from local installation toward remote hosted services, which changes how licensing works: cloud-based licensing is efficient enough that end users often need not navigate separate licence processes, making licensing effectively invisible from their perspective. That convenience does not remove the obligation to track what is in use.

Open-source software is pervasive in cloud, with developers relying on open-source applications, toolsets, and frameworks. What distinguishes the most widely used packages is the rigour of their development process, including peer review and active maintenance. Validated open source means components that have been assessed for provenance, maintenance status, and known vulnerabilities before adoption, rather than pulled in simply because they solve the immediate problem. Software Composition Analysis and a maintained Software Bill of Materials are the practical mechanisms for keeping that inventory current.

4.6 Cloud application architecture Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 6, Cloud Application Security (supplemental security devices, cryptography, sandboxing, and application virtualization sections).

Supplemental components, cryptography, sandboxing, and orchestration

Supplemental security components

Study guide: Ch 6 › Cloud Application Architecture › Supplemental Security Components

These devices augment a defence-in-depth architecture, where multiple overlapping layers of security mutually reinforce one another.

  • Web Application Firewall (WAF), a layer 7 firewall that understands HTTP traffic and can block application-layer attacks such as injection and cross-site scripting.
  • Database Activity Monitoring (DAM), a layer 7 monitoring device that understands SQL commands and detects anomalous or unauthorised database activity.
  • XML firewall, inspecting XML traffic and protecting against XML-specific attacks such as malformed documents and entity expansion.
  • API gateway, centralising authentication, rate limiting, and version management for APIs.
  • Load balancer, distributing traffic across backend instances, which supports availability and can terminate TLS.
Watch this in the exam

WAF understands HTTP, DAM understands SQL. Both operate at layer 7 but inspect different protocols, and questions frequently offer both. If the scenario concerns web request content, it wants a WAF; if it concerns database queries and access patterns, it wants DAM.

How the supplemental components fit together

These are complementary layers, not alternatives, and the clearest way to hold them is by what each inspects and where it sits. The network firewall filters at layer 3 and 4 on IP addresses and port numbers. The WAF sits in front of the application and filters malicious HTTP and HTTPS requests at layer 7 by inspecting the content of the request rather than only the packet headers, which makes it the primary control for injection and broken access control attempts. The API gateway governs API traffic specifically, handling authentication, rate limiting, request validation, and routing before traffic reaches back-end services, which addresses exactly the failure pattern behind large-scale API scraping. Database activity monitoring watches what happens at the database once a query gets through, understanding SQL and database protocols natively, and flags anomalous or unauthorised activity such as unusual exfiltration patterns, privilege escalation, or injection attempts that passed the WAF. XML security ensures data exchanged between services is well formed and safely parsed, which matters because XML carries its own vulnerability class including external entity attacks.

Two points follow. Database activity monitoring complements encryption and access control rather than duplicating them, by providing visibility into who is doing what with the data, and it feeds directly into the audit logging requirements from the data security domain. And placement and ownership shift with the service model: in IaaS the customer typically manages its own firewalls and security groups, while in SaaS the provider manages almost all of it, which makes this list a reliable vehicle for shared responsibility questions.

Watch this in the exam

Zero trust network access and secure access service edge are not part of this outline's supplemental components list, despite being central to current cloud security practice and arguably belonging here. Know the concepts, since zero trust appears elsewhere in the certification, but answer questions on supplemental security components from the list above rather than reaching for the architecture you would actually deploy.

Cryptography

Study guide: Ch 3 › Encryption; Ch 6 › Cloud Application Architecture

Cloud systems operate across trusted and untrusted networks, which can be described as hostile or semi-hostile environments, so encryption is essential for data stored within and transmitted between cloud services. Three encryption scopes are distinguished:

  • Whole instance encryption, encrypting all data associated with the operation and use of a virtual machine.
  • Volume encryption, encrypting a single volume on a drive.
  • File or directory encryption, encrypting an individual file or directory.

Sandboxing

Study guide: Ch 6 › Application Virtualization and Orchestration

Sandboxing is the segregation and isolation of information or processes within the same system or application for security purposes. It isolates different communities and user populations from one another's data, and the requirement can be driven internally by organisational policy or externally by regulation. It is also the standard technique for safely executing untrusted code or analysing suspected malware.

Two uses dominate in practice. The first is separation of data and user populations, keeping communities isolated from one another's data within a shared system. The second is safe execution of untrusted code: testing new code or deployments without exposing production systems, and detonating suspect files to observe their behaviour. Malware analysis is the clearest case, since running a sample anywhere near production risks exactly the spread the analysis is meant to prevent. Sandboxing becomes more important in cloud, not less, because physical network separation is generally unavailable as a fallback.

Application virtualisation and orchestration

Study guide: Ch 6 › Application Virtualization and Orchestration

Infrastructure virtualisation underpins cloud computing, while containerisation underpins many cloud application architectures including microservices. Containers separate corporate data and software from the user's device, which lets an organisation remotely wipe corporate data, applications, and access without touching the employee's personal content, a meaningful protection for both parties. Orchestration software manages containers and applications at scale and plays a critical role in enforcing security and compliance with the organisation's secure computing policies, letting customers oversee all their cloud resources from a central platform. Kubernetes is the dominant orchestration platform, and securing it, along with the container registry and the images themselves, is part of securing the application.

4.7 Identity and Access Management solutions Study guide reference: Maps to Chapter 6, Cloud Application Security (identity and access management, federated identity, and multifactor authentication sections).

Federation, identity providers, SSO, MFA, CASB, and secrets

Federated identity

Study guide: Ch 6 › Identity and Access Management › Federated Identity Management

Federated identity works much like single sign-on, with one digital identity granting access to multiple systems, but it extends across boundaries: a federated identity can reach applications across different cloud providers, multi-cloud resources, and on-premises systems, and it enables single sign-on across systems belonging to different organisations. Both the on-premises IAM system and the IAM systems of each cloud vendor must be closely safeguarded against unauthorised use. Federation carries the same drawback as SSO in amplified form: when one federated system is compromised, that compromise exposes vulnerabilities across all the federated systems.

The security trade-off is concentration of risk. If one digital identity is compromised, every federated platform that identity reaches is exposed, and the same applies if one member system in the federation is breached. Two properties make this harder than single sign-on inside one organisation: the member systems belong to different organisations that may not share the same level of trust, and no single organisation controls how the others secure their systems or validate their users. That is why continuous monitoring of identity usage, and a fast response to suspicious activity, matter more in a federation than in a self-contained environment, and why the on-premises IAM and each provider's IAM both need safeguarding against overlapping or unauthorised use.

Identity providers

Study guide: Ch 6 › Identity and Access Management › Identity Providers

An identity provider may be a provider's own service or a third-party service. In multi-cloud scenarios a third-party identity provider is often the most efficient choice, since it gives one unified solution across providers rather than several separate ones. Identity providers complement rather than replace existing cloud security technologies such as CASBs.

Single sign-on

Study guide: Ch 6 › Identity and Access Management › Single Sign-On

SSO streamlines access to all approved systems within a single IAM system after one authentication, letting users move between systems without re-authenticating. Because one credential now opens many doors, monitoring all access within an SSO system is critical: a compromised digital identity puts every connected system at risk, which is precisely why SSO is normally paired with strong authentication.

Multi-factor authentication

Study guide: Ch 6 › Identity and Access Management › Multifactor Authentication

MFA, also called strong authentication, adds verification layers by requiring at least two factors from different categories: something you know such as a password, something you have such as a token or phone, and something you are such as a fingerprint or other biometric. Two instances of the same category, such as a password and a security question, do not constitute genuine multi-factor authentication.

Example

A bank pairs SSO with mandatory MFA. SSO means one compromised password would otherwise reach every internal system, so the second factor is what keeps a phished credential from becoming a full breach. The convenience of SSO and the necessity of MFA are two sides of the same design decision.

Beyond the three factor categories, several mechanisms recur. One-time passwords, whether from a hardware token displaying a rotating code, a soft token application, or a push notification, are the familiar something-you-have implementations. Challenge questions and out-of-band authentication, where verification travels over a separate channel from the login attempt, add further layers. The joiners, movers, and leavers process is where much of this succeeds or fails in practice, particularly the mechanism for communicating a first login credential to a new user, which is a step often handled far less carefully than the authentication system it protects.

Cloud Access Security Broker

Study guide: Ch 1 › Cloud Computing Roles and Responsibilities; Ch 6 › Cloud Identity and Access Control

A CASB is a software product or service positioned between an organisation's on-premises infrastructure and a cloud provider's infrastructure. Acting as a gatekeeper, it lets the organisation extend its reach beyond its own network boundaries and enforce security policy within cloud environments. Typical CASB capabilities include visibility into cloud usage including unsanctioned shadow IT, data security enforcement such as DLP across sanctioned services, threat protection, and compliance monitoring.

The four pillars of a CASB

  • Visibility, discovering shadow IT: the unsanctioned cloud applications employees are using without the knowledge or approval of IT, which cannot be governed until they are known.
  • Compliance, checking cloud usage against regulatory requirements such as PCI DSS, HIPAA, and the GDPR, supporting data residency requirements by monitoring and controlling where data is stored and processed, and generating audit evidence and compliance reporting across services.
  • Data security, extending DLP into cloud services so sensitive data such as card numbers, intellectual property, and personal data can be detected and blocked before it is uploaded to or shared through sanctioned and unsanctioned applications alike.
  • Threat protection, detecting anomalous behaviour such as impossible travel logins, bulk downloads, and unusual access patterns that indicate compromised credentials or insider activity, scanning files for malware on upload and share, and applying adaptive access control that can step up authentication or block access outright based on real-time risk signals.

The four capability areas above are the industry framing of what a CASB delivers. The courseware presents the same product from the technology angle, listing firewalls, authentication, web application firewalls, and data loss prevention as the mechanisms a CASB typically brings. The two descriptions are the same thing viewed from different ends: authentication and adaptive access serve visibility and threat protection, DLP serves data security, and the reporting built on top serves compliance.

Watch this in the exam

A CASB does more than one job, which is what makes it a favourite for questions. If a scenario is about discovering unsanctioned applications, enforcing policy consistently across several providers, or producing compliance evidence spanning cloud services, the CASB is usually the answer. The exam may also describe the function without using the term, so recognise the capability rather than only the acronym.

Secrets, key, and certificate management

Study guide: Ch 6 › Identity and Access Management › Secrets Management

Cloud secrets management covers the methods and technologies used to safeguard digital credentials in cloud environments, with accessibility and authorisation as the key priorities, noting that each cloud platform implements secrets management differently. Cloud-based secrets include API keys, SSH keys, encryption keys, passwords, private certificates, and tokens. Centralised management enables rotation, auditing, and revocation in one place, replacing credentials embedded in code or configuration files.
